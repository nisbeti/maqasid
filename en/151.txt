Thus, the establishment of the principle of causation (ta'leel) by this group was nothing but theological discussions about the essence of God. Therefore, we see another group that categorically rejects this principle while affirming analogy (qiyas), causation, and reasoning through analogy (maslaha). Among the most famous of these is Fakhr al-Din al-Razi. He stated: "Analogy provides a presumption of causation, and it is obligatory to act upon presumption" (1). He also said: "It is established that analogy provides causation while being certain that the actions of Allah, Exalted is He, are not subject to causation" (2).
Al-Razi attempted to prove causation through the method of analogy, mentioning two approaches. The first is the method or evidence presented by al-Amidi. He mentioned it and then returned to refute it. As for the second, he affirmed it and supported it. He said: "Analogy provides a presumption of causation, and it is obligatory to act upon presumption. The explanation of the first (3) is from two angles: the first (4): that Allah, Exalted is He, legislated the rulings for the benefit of the servants, and this benefit leads to the presumption that Allah, Exalted is He, legislated it for this benefit. Thus, these are three premises that must be established with evidence. As for the first premise, the evidence for it is from various angles" (5). The reference in his statement "As for the first premise" is to his assertion that Allah, Exalted is He, legislated the rulings for the benefit of the servants, which refers to the principle of causation. He then presented six angles to establish this premise, which do not differ from what al-Amidi presented, but in a more detailed manner (6).
