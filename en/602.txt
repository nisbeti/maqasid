One of them is that the one who brings or pushes something is harmed by being prevented from that, so he is in need of doing it, like defending himself from an injustice that he knows will occur through someone else, or being the first to buy food or something he needs, or to hunt, gather firewood, or water, or anything else, knowing that if he acquires it, someone else will be harmed by its absence, and if it is taken from his hand, he will be harmed.
And the second is that he is not harmed by it, and this is of three types:
5. The first is what leads to corruption (fasad) in a definitive manner, meaning the ordinary certainty, like digging a well behind the door of a house in the dark such that anyone entering will inevitably fall into it, and similar situations.
6. The second is what leads to corruption rarely, like digging a well in a position that does not usually lead to anyone falling into it, and consuming foods that generally do not harm anyone, and similar matters.
And the third is what leads to corruption frequently, not rarely, and this is of two types:
7. One is what is predominant, like selling weapons to warring parties, and grapes to the drunkard, and anything that is sold by someone known for deceit, and so on.
8. The second is what is frequent but not predominant, like the issue of deferred sales.
So these are eight categories (1). Then al-Shatibi clarifies the ruling for each of these eight categories and establishes that the last two categories, the seventh and eighth, are where the principle of blocking the means (sadd al-dhara'i) is applied. 1 - The first category: Al-Shatibi says about it: "As for the first, it remains on its original basis of permission, and there is no doubt about it" (2).
