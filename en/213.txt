And if these objectives (maqasid) are the reason for the legislation of rulings, then the rulings are indeed legislated based on them, thus serving as the causes for legislation. The jurist (mujtahid) has no way to know the causes of the rulings except through following and deducing their consequences. For this reason, it is said that the objectives of the Sharia are to bring about benefits and to avert harms, and it is also said that the Sharia is characterized by bringing about benefits and averting harms.
Based on this, what applies to the cause applies to the consequence and to the wisdom mentioned earlier. Al-Shatibi said: "As for the cause, it refers to the wisdoms and benefits that are related to the commands or permissions, or the harms that are related to the prohibitions" (1). It follows that the cause must be definitive: it must be the benefit itself or the harm itself, not its mere possibility. He said: "In general, the cause is the benefit itself, or the harm, not its mere possibility, whether it is apparent or not, whether it is defined or not" (2). He said: "The cause may be known or unknown; if it is known, it is followed, and wherever it exists, the requirement of the command or prohibition exists, whether it is intended or not, such as marriage for the benefit of procreation, and sale for the benefit of benefiting from the contracted item, and punishments for the benefit of deterrence. The cause is recognized here through its known pathways in the principles of jurisprudence (usul al-fiqh). When it is determined, it is known that the intent of the Legislator is what those causes necessitate in terms of action or inaction, and in terms of causing or not causing. If it is unknown, one must refrain from asserting that the Legislator intended this or that" (3). Then he said: "And it is judged by knowledge or conjecture that it is not intended by Him (the Legislator). For if it were intended, there would have been a definitive proof for it" (4) (5).
