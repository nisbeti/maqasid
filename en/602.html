4. One of them is that the buyer or the defender incurs harm from being prevented from that action, so he is in need of performing it, like the one defending himself against an injustice that he knows will befall another, or rushing to purchase food or something he needs, or to hunt, or to gather firewood, or water, or anything else, knowing that if he obtains it, another will be harmed by its absence; and if it is taken from his hand, he will be harmed.

The second: that he does not incur harm from it, and this is of three types:

5. One of them is what leads to a certain harm, meaning a normal certainty, such as digging a well behind the door of the house in the dark, so that anyone entering will inevitably fall into it, and similar situations.

6. The second: that which its performance leads to harm rarely, such as digging a well in a manner that does not usually result in someone falling into it, and consuming foods that are mostly not harmful to anyone, and similar matters.

The third: what is performed that leads to harm frequently rather than rarely, and it has two aspects:

7. One of them is that it should be predominant, like the sale of weapons from the people of war, and grapes from the wine seller, and what is mixed with it by those whose nature is to deceive, and similar matters.

8. The second condition is that it should be abundant but not predominant, like the issue of deferred sales.

These are eight categories"(1).

Then al-Shatibi clarifies the ruling in each of these eight sections and establishes that the last two sections, the seventh and eighth, are where the principle of blocking the means (sadd al-dhara'i) is applied.
