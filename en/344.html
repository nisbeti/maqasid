The legal causes (musaabibat shar'iyya), meaning those intended by the Legislator, are those that have been established definitively, and this matter has been previously clarified. He said: "The legal rulings were established to bring about benefits and to avert harms, and they are certainly their causes" (1). Al-Shatibi affirms that: "The prohibited causes are causes of harms, not of benefits, just as the permissible causes are causes of benefits, not of harms" (2). He then states: "The point is that the permissible causes cannot be causes of harms, and the prohibited causes cannot be causes of benefits, as this is not valid in any case" (3).

What this statement means is that the cause that is established as intended to occur through the permissible cause is a benefit and not a harm, regardless of its suitability to human nature, sense, and disposition. Conversely, what is established as intended to be removed through the prohibited cause is a harm and not a benefit, even if it is something that human nature tends toward by virtue of instinct or habit, or something that rational individuals and wise people incline towards.

The legal correlation between the cause and its effect:

It is not valid for the legal ruling to be intended for anything other than what the Legislator intended, nor is it valid to achieve or intend what the Legislator intended through a ruling that was not legislated for that purpose. Al-Shatibi states: "The causes, as they are legal causes for their effects, were legislated to achieve their effects, which are the benefits sought or the harms averted" (4). He says: "The effects, in relation to their causes, are of two types: the first is what the causes were legislated for, either by the primary intention, which is related to the original objectives (maqasid al-sharia), or by the secondary intention, which is related to the subsidiary objectives...
