Thus, not everything that is based on a cause or observed in its attributes, rulings, or meanings that is acceptable to rational individuals takes the ruling of the caused. This leads to a question about how to distinguish between these results to know what is intended by the Sharia (law) to be a goal and an objective, and what is not, so that it can be disregarded. The answer lies in the method of induction for these attributes or meanings.

If there is a meaning or an imagined result presumed in a few rulings, this meaning or cause is not definitively considered intended by the legislator, and thus it is not taken into account, even if it is caused by these rulings; these rulings do not constitute a legal cause for it. However, if this result is found in numerous rulings that exceed enumeration, then this result or meaning has the continuity (tawatur) that al-Shatibi mentioned resembles the conceptual continuity. At that point, this result becomes a cause, and the rulings that support this meaning become a legal cause. That is, they are legislated for this cause. In this way, a distinction is made between legal and non-legal results or causes, or those that are intended by the legislator through the ruling and those whose intention by the legislator is not established.

If a ruling is legislated for specific interests that have been established through induction, and this ruling results in a harm, it does not mean that the existence of that harm as a cause of the ruling indicates that it is intended by the legislator. Rather, this is because interests and harms are not purely distinct. This result is recognized as a harm from its induction in many other rulings, where the beneficial induction is available to establish that the legislator intended to eliminate it. Thus, the legislator's intention to eliminate it is a definitive principle, and the existence of doubt or presumption in results, outcomes, or causes being intended by the legislator is a possibility or presumption that has no legal value. For the objectives or interests and harms are generalities or fundamental principles that can only be established through definitive evidence derived from induction.
