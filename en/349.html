... Al-Shatibi says: "The original ruling regarding acts of worship for the accountable person is to worship without considering the meanings" (1). This means that the original principle is that one should not seek a reason for them. His use of the term "original" indicates that there are rare or few cases where it is permissible to pursue a specific meaning to see if it is established or not. Al-Shatibi supports this with several points: "Among them is induction, for we have found that purification extends beyond its required place, and likewise, the prayers are specified with particular actions in specific forms; if they deviate from these, they are not acts of worship... And so it is with other acts of worship such as fasting and pilgrimage, and we understand from the general wisdom of worship the submission to the commands of Allah, Exalted is He, and the individual acts of submission and glorification of His majesty and turning to Him. This extent does not provide a specific reason from which a specific ruling can be understood" (2). There is no need to mention all his other proofs on this matter, and Al-Shatibi's intent is that acts of worship are not to be rationalized, and it is not permissible to seek a reason for them. Even if there are some rulings on acts of worship that can carry certain meanings that may be very appropriate and provoke thought, they should be disregarded and not considered, because the principle that acts of worship are not rationalized is a definitive principle established by induction and other matters. Thus, it is not correct to pursue this meaning in other rulings to prove it through induction. He states: "It is necessary to take in this category worship without considering the meanings as a principle built upon and a pillar to rely on" (3). As for the rare cases where it is permissible—though not obligatory—to follow the meaning, they are when a text or consensus indicates a specific meaning; this meaning is one of the meanings of the proofs and not one of the meanings of the rulings. He says: "Unless it is clarified by a text or consensus a desired meaning in some cases, there is no blame on one who follows it, but that is rare and not a principle; rather, the principle is what is prevalent in the matter and predominates in the context" (4). As for the fact that the intended meaning by a text or consensus differs from others, it is because it is one of the indications of the texts or their meanings, and not one of the indications of the rulings.
