Among the examples regarding conditions, he said: "If his action or inaction is intended as a condition to nullify the effect of the cause so that no effect results from it, then this is an invalid action and a futile endeavor" (1). He said: "This action renders what has been established as a cause for a legal ruling, whether for the sake of bringing about a benefit or preventing a harm, as mere play with no ruling or benefit. This contradicts what has been established in the principle of public interest (maslaha) and that it is considered in rulings. Furthermore, it opposes the intention of the Legislator in that when the cause has been established and exists, and is legally a cause for its effect, it is contingent upon the fulfillment of a condition that completes the cause. Thus, this actor or abstainer, intending to lift the ruling of the cause, is opposing the Legislator's establishment of the cause. It has become clear that opposing the intention of the Legislator is invalid; therefore, this action is void" (2).

Accordingly, if the obligated person acts with the intention of nullifying the ruling, then his action is blameworthy because it opposes the intention of the Legislator. However, if he acts for a legitimate reason of benefit, then it is permissible even if it results in nullifying a cause or changing a ruling, and this does not fall under the category of legal tricks (hiyal) at all. He said in response to those who claim the permissibility of legal tricks, such as one who spends his wealth or part of it before the due date to avoid the obligation of zakat: "This meaning only applies if he does not intend to lift the ruling of the cause; however, with the intention to do so, it is a meaning that is not considered because the Sharia has definitively annulled it" (3). He then said: "This is only because he has introduced a condition or lifted a condition that removes what the first cause necessitated, just as the one who spends his due amount with the intention of lifting what it necessitated regarding the obligation of giving" (4). He then said: "Thus, introducing conditions or lifting them with that intention is what is prohibited, and if it is prohibited, it opposes the intention of the Legislator and is therefore void" (5).
