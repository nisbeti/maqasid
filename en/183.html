The intention of the usuliyyin (principlists), before al-Shatibi, regarding the principles of jurisprudence (usul al-fiqh) that must be definitive is the Qur'an and the Sunnah, along with what they indicate from general sources, which are consensus (ijma) and analogy (qiyas). As for al-Shatibi, he does not limit the requirement of definitiveness to these sources; rather, the universals (kulliyat) for him are necessities (darurat), needs (hajiyat), and enhancements (tahsinat), as well as what complements them. If we understand that the necessities for him are the preservation of religion, intellect, life, lineage, and wealth, this indicates that these objectives must be definitive to be considered; one cannot suffice with mere conjecture or with them being based on definitive sources like the Qur'an and the Sunnah. Likewise, if we understand that the permissibility of marriage, the removal of harm, and the non-obligation of burdensome duties are among the needs for him, this means they must be established definitively to be considered. The same applies to the category of enhancements and complements. Once these meanings are established, they become principles, that is, sources for legislation in his view, and are used as evidence just as the legal texts are used.

If these meanings are not established definitively through induction, they must either return to a definitive origin or not. If they return to a definitive origin, there is no issue in employing them in legislation. If they do not return, there is a detailed discussion that will be mentioned in its place, if Allah wills. He said: "Every legal evidence is either definitive or conjectural. If it is definitive, there is no issue in considering it... If it is conjectural, it either returns to a definitive origin or does not; if it returns to a definitive origin, it is also considered, and if it does not return, one must be cautious regarding it, and it is not correct to simply accept it." (1)
