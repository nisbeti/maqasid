On this basis, al-Shatibi's position is established: is it permissible to act upon the general (al-am) before searching for a specific exception (mukhassas), or not? For the matter under consideration may be one that has been specified. He believes that if the general reaches the level of certainty (qat'), there is no need to search for a specific exception, as the general, if established—according to his methodology—this is through inductive reasoning (istiqra) that leads to certainty, and thus it does not contradict or lose its meaning in any situation or condition. However, if the texts that indicate the general do not come together in a way that leads to certainty, then the meaning requires further investigation and inquiry. This inquiry means searching for what agrees with or contradicts it, in order to reach the certain meaning and the limits of what this meaning encompasses. He states: "Generalities, if their meaning is unified and they spread across the branches of the Sharia or are repeated in contexts according to need without specification, they are to be applied in their generality in all cases, even if we say that specification by separation (al-takhssis bil-munfasal) is permissible" (1). His statement here, "even if we say that specification by separation is permissible," refers to before the meaning is repeated and established, and this will become clear in what follows. He says: "In general, every principle that is repeated in its affirmation and its matter is confirmed, and this is understood from the flow of speech, is taken according to its generality" (2). He then said: "As for if the general is neither repeated nor confirmed nor widespread in the branches of jurisprudence, then relying on it alone is questionable; it is necessary to search for what contradicts it or specifies it. The distinction between the two types arises because what has been repeated, confirmed, and widespread has its apparent meaning, supported by contextual evidence, elevated to the status of a definitive text (nass) that has no ambiguity, unlike what is not so, which is subject to possibilities, and thus one must refrain from making a definitive judgment based on it until it is compared with others and investigated for the existence of contradictions" (3).

What is mentioned here about the general and the specific applies in his view to the unrestricted (mutlaq) and the restricted (muqayyad), because universals (kulliyat) are characterized by being unrestricted and general.
