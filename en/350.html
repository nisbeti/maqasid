As for the fact that it is permissible to follow the meaning in this case but not obligatory, this is because this meaning is speculative and cannot be considered unless it is related to a definitive origin, and this can only be established through induction. Following the meaning may affirm it or negate it; if it negates it, then it is confirmed to adhere to the act of worship, and if it affirms it, then the origin of worship is also established by the same method, that is, through induction. Therefore, it has no effect on acts of worship, neither by establishment nor by negation. He says: "What is purely for Allah, like acts of worship, has its origin in the act of worship which is based on the incomprehensibility of the meaning, such that analogy cannot be applied to it. If its meaning is not comprehensible, it indicates that the intention of the Legislator is to adhere to what He has defined and not to exceed it" (1). As for the comprehensible meaning that has been established as intended by the Legislator, it must be applied in matters other than acts of worship. Moreover, although some acts of worship contain indications of reasoning or suggest a connection, the conditions for reasoning are either absent or incomplete in them. Therefore, such descriptions or meanings do not extend the rulings, such as theft being a reason for cutting off the hand and adultery being a reason for stoning; however, analogy does not occur here, nor does the ruling extend beyond its place. This is stronger in acts of worship, as in forgetfulness being a reason for prostration. We say: "He stole, so his hand was cut off; he committed adultery, so he was stoned; he forgot, so he prostrated." These reasons, while they are understood in general terms, are not understood in specific terms. The understanding is of the general crime or sin and the general punishment or excuse. However, specifying this with that is not comprehensible, as it could logically be said: "He stole, so he was imprisoned," or "He forgot, so he repeated," or something else. The same applies to reasoning based on hardship in shortening prayers, breaking the fast, and combining prayers. He says: "Moreover, the appropriateness in them (i.e., acts of worship) is counted among those that have no counterpart, such as hardship in the traveler's shortening of prayers, breaking the fast, and combining between the two prayers, and similar matters. To this, most of the understood reasons in the chapters of acts of worship are not understood in specific terms, like his saying: 'He forgot, so he prostrated'" (2).
