Al-Amidi said: "Most scholars have stated that the condition for the cause of the original ruling is that it should not be the subject of the original ruling nor a part of its subject. Others have permitted it, and the preferred view is to differentiate, which is that it is impermissible in the original but permissible in the part."(1) He also said: "If the cause were specifically the subject of the original ruling, then the cause would be limited... Yes, this is only possible if the cause of the original ruling is not transitive."(2) He added: "As for the part, it is not impermissible to use it as a cause due to the possibility of the generality of the original and the branch."(3)

Al-Zarkashi said: "If it is transitive, meaning it exists outside the original, it is required that the causation in the subject be only a part of it. It cannot be imagined to be transitive, unlike the limited, where this is permissible."(4)

The Third Topic
The Path of Analogy and Appropriateness

Introduction:
Previously, the concept of appropriateness was mentioned as a condition of the cause, which is a pillar in analogy (qiyas). Some of the usul scholars have made it a distinct path of causation with its own conditions. The following discusses the topic of appropriateness, its conditions, and its categories.
The reasoning based on the appropriate description is founded on the principle that the original rulings are based on reasoning. If the Sharia indicates the cause through explicit or implicit text, consensus, or by deduction through hints and indications, then that is sufficient. If it does not indicate, one must search for the most suitable description for reasoning. The most suitable is the one that is most appropriate, provided that the Sharia does not indicate its rejection or abrogation. The appropriate description is one that results in a benefit or harm depending on whether the ruling is a command for action or a prohibition against it.
However, determining the appropriate beneficial description in the ruling is influenced by the jurist's perspective on what constitutes benefit and what constitutes harm, as well as their view on the issue of goodness or badness in actions. A jurist may perceive a benefit that later, upon examination and research, turns out to be a harm, and vice versa, or that the Sharia has abrogated or invalidated it, or that there is something more beneficial and more appropriate for reasoning.
