The answer - according to his methodology - is that what is originally permissible serves a necessary, need-based, or enhancement principle, and the prohibited outcome is also a servant to a necessary, need-based, or enhancement principle, and its occurrence is contrary to the occurrence of the original permissible. If the permissible action - which is a cause - and the prohibited outcome are both referring to or serving the same original principle, then this is not a place for applying the principle of blocking the means (sadd al-dhara'i), because the prohibition pertains to the complements of the original and not to the original itself, while the permissible refers to the original. Therefore, it is preferable not to consider what pertains to the complement, and it has been established in the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) that the collapse of the complement does not destroy the original, whereas the collapse of the original does destroy its complements. Thus, here too, the permissible remains permissible and is not affected by incidental matters. However, if the permissible and the prohibited outcome refer to two different originals, then this is something where the outcome may be considered, but it is not in the context of blocking the means; rather, it is in the context of conflict and preference.
Al-Shatibi, responding to the aforementioned question, says: "The absence of incidental matters concerning this original must either be a complement to it in its category or from another category that is an original in itself. If it is the latter, then it is either actual or anticipated. If it is anticipated, it has no effect in the presence of hardship, because hardship from abandonment is actual and is a corruption, while the corruption of the incidental matter is anticipated and imagined, thus it does not oppose the actual at all. As for if it is actual, it is truly a matter of ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning), and the corruption of the incidental matters therein may be greater than the corruption of abandoning the permissible, and the matter may be the opposite, and the consideration here pertains to conflict and preference. If it is the first case, then there is no valid conflict nor equivalence of the two corruptions, rather the corruption of losing the original is greater, and the evidence for this is as follows: one, that the complement with its complement is like the attribute with the attributed" (1). He then said: "And if the loss of the attribute does not return with the loss of the attributed in general, unlike the opposite, the side of the attributed is stronger in existence and non-existence" (2). "And the second is that the original with its complements is like the universal with the particular, and it is known that when the universal is opposed by the particular, the particular has no effect; likewise here, the corruption of losing the complement has no effect in the presence of the benefit of the complemented" (3).
Thus, the first criterion for applying the principle is established, which is "that one should not be compelled to the original permissible nor should hardship be attached to abandoning it; this is a place for ijtihad and the principle of blocking the means applies here" (4).
As for his statement: "this is a place for ijtihad," it indicates that in this case there are two opinions: one considers the original permissibility and does not consider the opposing incidental matter, while the other considers the opposing incidental matter and negates the permissibility to block the means.
