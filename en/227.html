He says: "If it is accepted that the purpose of the Sharia (law) is for the interests of the servants, then it returns to them according to the command of the Lawgiver and within the limits He has set, not according to their whims and desires. Therefore, the legal obligations are burdensome on the souls, and the senses, customs, and experience bear witness to this. The commands and prohibitions remove him from the inclinations of his nature and the pursuit of his desires until he takes them from beneath the prescribed limit... As for the fact that the interests of the obligations return to the obligated person in both the immediate and the delayed sense, this is correct, and it does not necessitate that attaining them be outside the limits of the Sharia or that he obtains them by himself without the Sharia providing them to him, and this is evident"(1). He says: "The pursuit of interests and desires does not contradict the purpose of the Sharia from this perspective because the Sharia is also established for the interests of the servants. If the interest is made subordinate, there is no harm on the doer, except that there is a significant condition here, which is that the means by which he achieves his interest must be shown to be legislated by the Lawgiver for the attainment of such, otherwise the prior matter is the command of the Lawgiver"(2).

He says: "The actions and abstentions, in terms of being actions or abstentions, are rationally equivalent regarding their intended purpose, as there is no rational basis for commendation or condemnation. When the legislator specifies one of the equivalents for the sake of public interest (maslaha) and designates the other for harm (mafsada), he clarifies the reason through which the public interest is achieved, thus commanding it or permitting it. He also clarifies the reason through which harm is achieved, thus prohibiting it out of mercy for the servants. If the obligated person intends exactly what the legislator intended by permission, he has aimed for the public interest in its most complete form, and he is deserving of it being realized for him. However, if he intends something other than what the legislator intended, this often occurs due to a misconception that the public interest lies in what he intended, as a rational person does not intentionally aim for harm. He has thus rendered what the legislator intended as disregarded, while considering what the legislator neglected as a valid aim, which is a clear contradiction to the Sharia."(3)
