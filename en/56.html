The cause (illah) is a pillar of analogy (qiyas) and is not valid without it. It is the common description between the original case (asl) and the branch case (far') that allows for the transfer of the ruling from the original to the branch. Al-Zarkashi said: "The cause: it is a condition for the validity of analogy, as it brings together the original and the branch" (1), and he said: "The majority of the scholars of analogy among the jurists and theologians hold that the cause is necessary in analogy, and it is a pillar of analogy that cannot stand without it" (2). This is the legal cause. The term "cause" is a shared term between the rational cause (illah 'aqliyya) and the legal cause (illah shar'iyya), and it has linguistic and technical connotations.

In terms of language, it is mentioned in Lisan al-Arab: "The cause (al-'ilal) and the ailments (al-'alal): the second drink, and it is said: drinking after drinking successively; it is said: he drank (alal) after he quenched his thirst (nahal)" (3), and it is also mentioned: "The man suffers (ya'il) from illness, and he suffers (ya'lu) from the ailments of drinking" (4).

Al-Zarkashi said: "The cause in language is said to be a name for what changes the ruling of a thing upon its occurrence, derived from the ailment which is the disease, because its effect on the ruling is like the effect of the cause on the essence of the sick person" (5), and he said: "It is said that it is a transfer of the ruling from the original to the branch, like the transition of the cause from health to illness" (6). He also said: "It is said that it is derived from the ailments after quenching, which is the repeated drinking of water again and again, because the jurist in extracting it revisits the matter after consideration, and because the ruling is repeated with the repetition of its existence" (7).

As for the technical definition in legal terminology, that is, the cause of legal analogy, there are five opinions:

"The first is that it is the identifier of the ruling, meaning that it has become a sign of the ruling; if the meaning is found, the ruling is found" (8).

"The second is that it is the cause of the ruling in the sense that the legislator has made it obligatory in itself" (9).
