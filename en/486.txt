Therefore, al-Shatibi's example of this type of clarification has a specificity that aligns with his methodology. He said: "An example of this aspect is what was mentioned at the beginning of the book of legal proofs regarding the meaning of his saying (peace and blessings be upon him): 'There should be neither harming nor reciprocating harm' (1) from the book" (2). The meaning is that the hadith must refer back to a definitive source in the Qur'an, otherwise it is not accepted. By referring to the verses of the Qur'an that prohibit harm, a general principle emerges that elevates harm, and thus the hadith can be applied without deviating from being a clarification; this meaning falls under the intention he seeks, and it is indeed the secret of his methodology (3).
The sixth point is that the Sunnah clarifies what is in the Qur'an without adding further explanation or detail to it. The Qur'an contains the explanation of everything, including the number of units of prayer and the times of prayers, the details of zakat, and similar matters. All of this is present in the Qur'an, but knowledge of it requires scholars who understand and delve into the secrets of the meanings of the Qur'an. Al-Shatibi's apparent statement refutes this meaning. He said: "Among them is the consideration of the details of the hadiths in the details of the Qur'an, and although the Sunnah provides additional clarification, the holder of this view seeks to find every meaning in the Sunnah indicated linguistically, not from another perspective or explicitly stated in the Qur'an" (4). He then said: "However, the Qur'an does not fulfill this purpose based on the Arabic text and indications that the Arabs use or similar" (5). He said: "Thus, the one who adheres to this does not fulfill what he claims unless he makes efforts in sources that the speech of the Arabs does not accept, nor does it align with what the righteous predecessors or the scholars firmly grounded in knowledge would agree upon" (6).
