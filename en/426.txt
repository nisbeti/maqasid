Abrogation (naskh) can only occur in particulars that relate to universals, that is, in branches of law, because if they are not related to universals, then their application has not been established, and what has not been established cannot be subject to abrogation. Likewise, the abrogated (mansukh) in relation to the definitive (muhkam) in Islamic law is few. He said: "For the induction shows that the particular branches in which abrogation and abrogated occurred in relation to what remains definitive are few" (1). It is noted in this text his use of the expression "particular branches."
Just as the abrogated must have been established in the law, so too must the abrogating, and its establishment must relate to a definitive meaning. He said: "If the rulings have been established upon the obligated person, then the claim of abrogation in them can only be made with a verified matter, because their establishment upon the obligated person is first verified, thus lifting them after knowledge of their establishment can only be with a verified knowledge" (2).
Since what was revealed in the law in Mecca consists of universals, and what pertains to branches is few, therefore, in ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning) and istinbat (deduction), there should be a tendency to exclude abrogation regarding what was revealed in Mecca. He then generalized this statement to all rulings, whether they are Meccan or Medinan. He said: "This necessitates that what was among the Meccan rulings claimed to be abrogated should not have that claim accepted except with a decisive proof of abrogation such that it is impossible to reconcile between the two evidences nor to claim definitiveness in both" (3). He then said: "And thus it is said regarding all rulings, whether Meccan or Medinan" (4).
