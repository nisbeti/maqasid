Therefore, the reason can sometimes be a cause, but it should be noted that there is a difference between them in legal terminology. The reason exists for the ruling to be present at it, not by it, whereas the cause exists for the ruling to be present at it and by it. Both can be so (i.e., a reason or a cause) by the designation of the legislator. If the term "cause" is used for the reason without it being a cause, meaning the element of analogy, this is a case of linguistic metaphor and not that it is a cause that justifies the rulings. Al-Amidi said: "The preferred view is that the cause must be, in essence, a motivator, meaning it must include a valid wisdom that is intended by the legislator in establishing the ruling. Otherwise, if it were a description without wisdom, merely an indication, then justifying rulings by it in essence is impossible" (1). He also said: "What they mentioned regarding the impossibility of justifying by the descriptive characteristic is only valid if it is said that justifying by the descriptive characteristic means the motivator" (2). The connection between Al-Amidi's statements above indicates that he permits the use of the term "cause" for mere indications even if they are not the element of analogy.

Moreover, it should be noted after distinguishing between the cause and the reason in legal terminology that the cause in legal terminology is a reason in the linguistic sense. The fact that the cause is the motivator for the ruling or the reason for the ruling means that it leads to the ruling, which is the meaning of the ruling encompassing a rational meaning, or a meaning valid to be intended by the legislator in establishing the ruling (3).

Maqsad:

Maqsad in the language is the name of the place of connection, and connection means hanging. It is said in Lisan al-Arab: "To connect something means to hang it. The connection is what is hung" (4).
