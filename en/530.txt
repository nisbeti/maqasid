The third description, which is to guide people towards the middle ground, is that people differ in the extent of their ability to bear obligations and hardships, in their inclination towards acts of obedience and their detachment from worldly matters, and in their relinquishment of interests and rights, or in their pursuit of worldly gains and their insistence on their rights and efforts towards their interests. However, the legislator has set limits for everyone regarding the obligations they must fulfill and the prohibitions they must avoid. Therefore, one who renounces worldly matters and his interests and rights is not permitted to neglect the preservation of his own self and body, nor to obtain what is necessary for his sustenance and that of his family and those whom the legislator has entrusted him to care for and provide for, and so on; he cannot abandon these responsibilities in favor of worship, for example. Likewise, one who is inclined towards the world and its adornments, and what Allah has permitted him from it, has obligations that he must fulfill and rights that he must uphold. Between these two extremes, people vary in their inclination towards permissible and recommended actions and their distance from disliked actions, or their abstention from them, and the state of people in this regard is not uniform. They also differ in their physical and mental capabilities, just as they differ in their spiritual and material motivations. Based on this, it is not permissible for the mufti to exceed the limits of obligations or prohibitions in his fatwa, and what lies between these limits constitutes the middle ground, where what is suitable for one obligated person may not be suitable for another. The assessment of the state of the obligated person returns to the specific determination of the relevant criteria, which has been clarified in the previous discussion. Some obligated individuals may take themselves to task with strict resolutions, distancing themselves from concessions, and they may treat recommended actions as if they were obligatory and abandon disliked actions as if they were prohibited, while such resolutions and recommendations may be burdensome for others, and neglecting disliked actions may be detrimental to them and could lead to sins. The knowledgeable and grounded mufti issues a fatwa that is appropriate for the state of the obligated person in what the law has allowed for flexibility, and when he issues a fatwa concerning a public interest or a matter affecting the community or the public, he guides them towards the middle ground, that is, not according to the state of the least capable among them nor according to the state of the most capable.
