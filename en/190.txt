Every objective must be absolute and definitive, and it must serve and affirm the necessary objectives, which are: the preservation of religion, life, intellect, lineage, and wealth. The enhancements (tahsinat) serve the needs (hajiyat), and both serve the necessities (darurat).
The legal rulings must return to one of the enhancing, need-based, or necessary principles, meaning that the objective of every ruling must support or serve a benefit that has been established through comprehensive and definitive induction in one of the three categories. If the ruling does not appear to return to any principle, it is either to be rejected or suspended.
The meaning of the Sharia (law) coming for the interests of the servants is that there is no ruling that does not return with a benefit for the servants, even if it does not entail harm; the mere conception of such a ruling implies that it is frivolous, and there is no frivolity in the Sharia.
The action required by the Sharia, whether obligatory or recommended, serves a comprehensive principle or more than one of the principles that are the objectives of the Sharia. The action prohibited by the Sharia, whether forbidden or disliked, undermines or serves to undermine a comprehensive principle or more than one of the principles that are the objectives or interests.
With these principles or rules of the concept of objectives, al-Shatibi's discussion of the permissible (mubah) is directed. If it is said that the permissible is what the legislator has left to choice between action and abandonment, or it is what neither the doer is blamed nor praised for, nor is the abandoner blamed or praised, or it is what is equal in the eyes of the legislator regarding action and abandonment, or what the textual evidence indicates regarding the legislator's address of choice between action and abandonment, or something similar to these statements, then this is problematic according to those rules, as the action has a wisdom and abandoning it forfeits that wisdom. If the intended wisdom is in abandoning the action, then performing it forfeits it. If the rulings were legislated only for the benefit of the servants, there must be a preference for one side over the other, or it must be claimed that there exists a fourth category beyond the enhancements that is characterized by not serving any of the three categories nor undermining any of them. However, this claim contradicts al-Shatibi's idea that no legal ruling is devoid of an objective, whether by bringing about a benefit or averting harm. So how does al-Shatibi resolve this problem?
