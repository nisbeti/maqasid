What also indicates this is what he said when discussing the ranks of scholars (1), where he categorized them into three ranks: the first is one whose understanding of the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) has not yet been established, so his ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning) is not valid, and he must refrain and follow (taqlid). The third is the rank of those firmly grounded in knowledge, and there is no disagreement regarding the validity of ijtihad from such a person. The second rank is the one in question here, and it indicates the sufficiency of ignoring the particulars.

He described the characteristics of the person in this second rank of knowledge, summarizing that he has a complete and thorough understanding of the general principles of the Sharia, but when he engages in ijtihad, he applies the general principles and does not engage with the particulars. Al-Shatibi then questioned whether a person in this rank is capable of valid ijtihad or not, stating that this is a matter of consideration and ambiguity, and it is a point of contention. He then mentioned the arguments of those who permit his ijtihad and the arguments of those who prohibit it, concluding that the issue remains problematic.

The evidence that this methodology necessitates the validity of neglecting the particulars consists of two points:

First: His statement that the issue remains problematic, and based on his assertions regarding the obligation to apply both particulars and general principles together, he should have sided with the prohibitors without hesitation. However, he did not do so, which suggests that the methodology aligns with neglecting the particulars for one who has completed the general principles. Likewise, he did not side with the permitters, as this would contradict his statements about the necessity of applying both particulars and general principles together, which led to claims of his inconsistency on this matter.

Second: All the arguments of the prohibitors are refuted according to his methodology and his texts, as he asserts that the general principles are definitive and inductive, thus they cannot be opposed, violated, or specified. Nevertheless, he did not refute the arguments of the prohibitors here; if he had, it would contradict what he previously established about the necessity of applying the particulars, as they all revert to the neglect of the particulars by the person in this rank.
