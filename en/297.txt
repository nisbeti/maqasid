... It has been established that true hardship is the reason for concession. However, since hardship is relative and people differ in their ability to bear it, the Sharia has specified a number of concessions that exist when their causes are present, such as the concessions of shortening prayers, combining prayers, and breaking the fast while traveling. These concessions are deemed permissible in general according to al-Shatibi. However, if there is a significant hardship, taking the concession becomes obligatory, even though it is still referred to as a concession. He said: "There is no doubt that the concession here is akin to decisive commands, and for this reason, the scholars have stated that it is obligatory to eat carrion out of fear of death; if one does not do so and dies, he will enter the Fire" (1). Hardship is divided into two categories: habitual and non-habitual. The former is not intended to be alleviated by the Sharia, while the latter requires a concession to be taken. He said: "When the hardship faced by the obligated person in fulfilling the obligation is beyond the usual hardships encountered in ordinary actions, to the extent that it leads to religious or worldly corruption, the aim of the Legislator in this case is to generally alleviate it" (2). The action that causes this hardship is prohibited, and it must either be completely removed or alleviated.
