This does not only pertain to cases of necessity or hardship but also to what serves a necessary or need-based purpose, such as marriage, which necessitates the sustenance of dependents and may lead to obtaining sustenance through what Allah has forbidden, as well as walking to mosques and in public places where the walker may encounter hearing forbidden things and seeing nakedness. Preventing marriage and walking in the markets places the obligated person in a hardship that the legislator has alleviated and leads to the prohibition of what is originally permissible. He said: "What is originally permissible due to necessity or need is subject to opposing circumstances that may affect the original permissibility, either in reality or in expectation, whether it reverts to the original permissibility through negation or not? This is a matter of consideration and difficulty. The ruling on it is that it is either that one is compelled to that permissible act or not, and if one is not compelled, then either one experiences hardship from leaving it or not. These are three categories. The first is that one is compelled to perform that permissible act, so it is necessary to revert to that original and not consider the opposing circumstance" (1). "The second category is that one is not compelled to it but experiences hardship from leaving it, so the consideration necessitates reverting to the original permissibility and disregarding the emergencies, as the prohibited things have been permitted to alleviate hardship" (2). As for the third category, "it is that one is not compelled to the original permissibility and does not experience hardship from leaving it; this is a matter of ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning), and the principle of means (dhara'i) applies based on the original cooperation on obedience or disobedience" (3).
However, al-Shatibi elaborates on the second category, which is when leaving the permissible act places the obligated person in hardship, as the hardship in undertaking actions or refraining from them is not of a single degree, and the Sharia has not come to remove all hardship; the harm of the hardship resulting from prohibiting the permissible may be less than the harm of engaging in the prohibited outcome. Al-Shatibi questions: "Does the hardship that follows from leaving the original act equate to the hardship that follows from engaging with the opposing circumstances or not?" (4).
