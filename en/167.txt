He said: "The principle, in any case, must be certain, because if it is merely probable, the possibility of disagreement arises, and such a thing cannot serve as a principle in religion based on induction" (1).
He concluded this introduction by saying: "Probabilities do not constitute principles, and this is sufficient to dismiss the uncertain from the principles outright; whatever is based on it that is not certain is derived from the certain" (2).
His statement in the text above: "And by the universals here, I mean the necessities, the needs, and the embellishments" indicates that he does not limit the condition of certainty in the principles of jurisprudence to the sources of legislation such as the Qur'an, the Sunnah, consensus (ijma), and analogy (qiyas).
He states in the second introduction: "The premises used in this science (3), and the evidence relied upon in it must be certain, because if they were merely probable, they would not yield certainty in the specific matters pertaining to it, and this is clear" (4). This is because the premises are the foundations or criteria through which, by linking them together or building upon them, one reaches the principles. Since the principles must be certain, their premises must also be certain.
