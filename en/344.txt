The legal causes (al-masbabat al-shar'iyya), meaning those intended by the Legislator, are those that have been established definitively, and this matter has been previously explained. He said: "The legal rulings were established to bring about benefits and avert harms, and they are certainly their causes"(1).
Al-Shatibi states that: "The prohibited causes are causes of harm, not of benefit, just as the permissible causes are causes of benefit, not of harm"(2). He then says: "The point is that the permissible causes cannot be causes of harm, and the prohibited causes cannot be causes of benefit, as this is not valid in any case"(3).
What this statement means is that the cause which is established as intended to occur through a permissible reason is a benefit (maslaha) and not a harm (mafsada), regardless of its suitability to human nature, perception, and disposition. And what is established as intended to be removed through a prohibited reason is a harm (mafsada) and not a benefit (maslaha), even if it is something that human nature tends to favor by virtue of instinct or habit, or if it is something that rational individuals and wise people incline towards.
The legal connection between the cause and its effect:
It is not correct for the legal ruling to be intended for anything other than what the legislator intended, nor is it correct to achieve or intend what the legislator intended through a ruling that was not legislated for that purpose. Al-Shatibi says: "The causes, inasmuch as they are legal causes for their effects, were legislated to achieve their effects, which are the benefits sought or the harms repelled"(4). He also says: "The effects, in relation to their causes, are of two types: the first type: those for which the causes were legislated either with the primary intention, which pertains to the original objectives (maqasid al-sharia), or with the secondary intention, which pertains to the subsidiary objectives...
