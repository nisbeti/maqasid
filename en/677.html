However, al-Shatibi explicitly responds to this objection and asserts the necessity of considering both the particular and the general together, saying: "The result is that it is essential to take into account the particulars while considering their universals, and vice versa, and this is the ultimate perspective of the jurists in general, and to this their opinions in the aims of ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning) ultimately lead" (1).

Yet, the objection raised against al-Shatibi here is valid; the disregard for particulars is necessary for the methodology, even though al-Shatibi stated his rejection of it. In fact, some of what he said indicates this disregard, and it appears that al-Shatibi recognized this matter as a logical consequence of his methodology, leading him to become confused and to struggle with the issue, hesitating to dismiss opinions by neglecting particulars, which will become clear. Among the indications of this matter is his statement regarding the validity of ijtihad by someone who understands the objectives (maqasid) both generally and in detail, even if he does not have knowledge of Arabic. He said: "Thus, whoever understands the objectives of the Sharia from the establishment of rulings and reaches a level of knowledge of them, even if his understanding comes through translation in a non-Arabic tongue, there is no difference between him and one who understands them through the Arabic tongue" (2). He also said: "If ijtihad is related to deriving rulings from texts, then knowledge of Arabic is a necessary condition; however, if it pertains to the meanings of benefits and harms that are independent of the requirements of the texts or are accepted by the one exercising ijtihad in the texts, then knowledge of Arabic is not required in that case. Rather, knowledge of the objectives of the Sharia in general and in detail is what is necessary" (3). Thus, this jurist does not know Arabic, so he cannot consider the particulars; he does not examine the texts nor derive from them, but he only applies the general principles on the condition that he has knowledge of them in general and in detail. Knowledge of Arabic is a condition for the jurist who derives from the particulars in order to reach an understanding of the general principles, and unless he attains knowledge of the general principles in general and in detail, he cannot engage in ijtihad, whether he is knowledgeable in Arabic or not.
