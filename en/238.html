How can one determine whether the objective (maqsad) is of the rank of necessities, needs, or enhancements? We have previously clarified the limits of each rank and provided examples for them. Nevertheless, the reader may wonder what makes pilgrimage (hajj), prayer (salah), or jihad (struggle) classified as necessities. Are they not things that, if neglected, would disrupt life, lead to chaos, and cause life to be lost? There are large societies in the world that do not observe these matters, and yet the feared consequences have not occurred. There are countries and communities that permit fornication, usury, and drinking alcohol, even though prohibiting these is necessary for the preservation of lineage, wealth, and intellect. What then makes, for example, the prohibition of pork and carrion classified as enhancements, or certain animals impure, or alcohol impure, while avoiding impurities is considered an enhancement? Yet, there are people who consume pork and carrion, live in the same place as dogs, drink alcohol, and use alcohol-based perfumes, without finding this to be something that rational minds would reject or that souls would abhor.

Similarly, how can fasting (sawm) be considered a necessity, while the lifting of obligations due to hardships or what is beyond one's capacity is classified as a need? Why is it not the other way around?

Al-Shatibi's answer to this matter is that it is a legal (shari) determination, not a rational one. Just as an objective cannot be classified as such unless the law indicates it through beneficial induction leading to certainty, the rank of the objective is also determined by the law, and this will be clarified with texts from him. However, there is a flawed perspective in viewing these objectives, which is based on identifying objectives or interests according to the prevailing concepts in society, or based on the concepts held by those with insight, knowledge, and experience, or decisions in society. These concepts may be influenced by legal rulings or may not be, and in this regard, these objectives are considered interests and deemed legal, upon which legislation is built. Those who contribute to or serve these objectives are considered legal, while those who undermine them are deemed corrupt and prohibited or unlawful. Al-Shatibi has repeatedly refuted this understanding in his Muwafaqat, yet there are still those who advocate it and attribute it to the law and to al-Shatibi.
