One example of this approach is when the legislator mentions a description that is appropriate alongside the ruling, such as his saying (peace and blessings be upon him): "The judge should not rule while he is angry"(1). This indicates that anger is a reason that prevents judgment due to the confusion of thought and disturbance of state it causes(2). The judge's role is to render judgment, and his sitting is for the purpose of judgment; thus, if he is prevented from judging in a certain state, this indicates that this state is the reason for the prohibition.

This section corresponds to the second section of the two divisions of the explicit text's indication of reasoning, but that is from the stated meaning (manṭūq) and this is from the implied meaning (mafhūm).

These three paths: consensus (ijma), textual evidence (nass), and indication and hint (tanbih wa imā') are agreed upon among the jurists. As for the subsequent paths, they are subject to disagreement; the proponents of the method of the theologians among the usuliyyin, specifically the Imams of the Shafi'i school, accept and defend them. Conversely, the proponents of the method of the jurists among the usuliyyin, namely the Hanafis, reject them.

The Shafi'i scholars refer to the reasons that come with these three pathways as the stated reasons (al-‘ilal al-mansusah), while the reasons that come with the other pathways that will follow are referred to as the inferred reasons (al-‘ilal al-mustanbatah). As for the Hanafi scholars, they mean by the inferred reasons (al-‘ilal al-mustanbatah) the reasons that come specifically with the pathways of indication and hinting, while the stated reasons (al-‘ilal al-mansusah) for them are those that come with the pathways of consensus (ijma) and text (nass).

The fourth approach: exploration and division. Some usul scholars have advocated for it. It is based on the premise that the foundation of rulings is reasoning. Its meaning is that the observer must thoroughly investigate all the attributes that could potentially be the reason for the ruling—based on the assumption that every ruling must have a reason. This is the exploration, then he examines these potential attributes one by one and establishes that they are not the reason for the ruling except for one of them, which is the division. Thus, the attribute that remains is the reason.
