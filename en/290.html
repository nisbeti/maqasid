He said: "If the purpose of the Sharia (law) were to impose hardship and difficulty, and it has been established that it is intended for ease and facilitation, then combining the two would be a contradiction and a discrepancy, and it is free from that." (1)

Difficulties are evils:

Accordingly, the legislator intends for ease and facilitation for the obligated person, and aims to eliminate difficulties. According to his methodology, difficulties are evils that must be removed, and it is forbidden for the obligated person to intend them for their own sake. He says: "The obligated person should not intend difficulty in the obligation due to the great reward for it; rather, he may intend the action that has a great reward because of its great difficulty." (2) And he said: "Nothing from the objectives of the obligated person is valid except what aligns with the intention of the legislator. If the intention of the obligated person is to impose difficulty, then he has contradicted the intention of the legislator, since the legislator does not intend the difficulty itself in the obligation. Any intention that contradicts the intention of the legislator is void; thus, the intention towards difficulty is void, and it is therefore among the things that are prohibited. What is prohibited has no reward in it; rather, it incurs sin if the prohibition rises to the level of being forbidden. Seeking reward by intending to enter into difficulty is a contradictory intention." (3) And he said: "His prohibition of hardship is well-known in the Sharia to the extent that it has become a definitive principle in it. If the intention of the legislator is not to impose hardship on the self, then the intention of the obligated person towards it is opposed to what the legislator intended by the known and established alleviation. If his intention contradicts the intention of the legislator, it is void and invalid. This is clear, and may Allah grant success." (4) And he said: "It has been made known by the legislator that difficulty is prohibited." (5) However, this raises a question: difficulties are inherent to human actions, and there is no obligation without difficulty. How can it be consistent that the legislator intends to eliminate difficulties while also requiring adherence to obligations? The answer from al-Shatibi, according to his methodology, is that the legislator's intention to eliminate difficulties is a general principle that does not fail.
