The meanings of rulings do not exist; rather, there are meanings of words and sentences that these words and sentences indicate in any type of indication (1). Therefore, once the meanings are known, there is no point in searching for meanings for them, nor is it correct to say that these meanings are intended by the legislator, for if they were intended by him, he would have established a proof for them or set a sign for them. At that point, they would be among the indications of the texts, and there is no harm in letting the mind or imagination roam in the reasons for the rulings, their causes, or their objectives and wisdom, especially since there is something in the soul that drives one to this, leading to the assumption of meanings or rulings that one becomes assured of or finds relief in. However, it is not permissible in any case to attribute any legal value to these assumptions, or to ascribe them to Allah - Exalted is He - by saying they are intended by Him, or that they are a cause for His ruling. Such assumptions are mere imaginations and are not based on any legal source, and since there is no evidence for them, they hold no significance. Al-Shatibi does not disagree with this; rather, he affirms it, and he does not accept legal assumptions at all unless they are based on a definitive meaning. Such justifications for rulings are assumptions that hold no value, but if the rulings converge on a meaning and there is a consensus, the meaning becomes definitive and serves as a benefit, cause, principle, and objective, all in a definitive manner. The objection is how this meaningful consensus will occur, or how the convergence of individual rulings on this meaning will happen, since there is no legal value for this meaning in any ruling, as rulings do not indicate meanings, and these meanings are not among the indications of the texts.

(1) Linguistic indication is limited to three types: correspondence, inclusion, and implication. The first: the indication of the word on all that it was designated for. The second: its indication of part of the named entity. The third: its indication of what is outside the named entity but is necessary for it. See: al-Zarkashi, al-Bahr al-Muhit, 1/417 and 1/420.
