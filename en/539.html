Al-Shatibi addresses the fatwas of those who are lenient and follow the concessions of the schools of thought, arguing that the Sharia is accommodating. He cites their statement, among which is: "His saying (peace and blessings be upon him): 'I was sent with the lenient Hanifiyyah'" (1) implies the permissibility of that because it is a type of kindness towards the servant, and the Sharia was not intended to cause hardship to the servants but to achieve benefits" (2). He responds by saying: "And you know from what has preceded in this discussion that the leniency of the Hanifiyyah is only found within the bounds of its principles, and that following concessions or choosing opinions based on desire is not established from its foundations; what has been said is merely a claim. Then we say: Following concessions is a deviation aligned with the whims of the souls, and the Sharia has come with a prohibition against following desires" (3).

As for leniency under the pretext of necessity or need where there is no necessity, he responds by reiterating the hadith regarding the lack of religion and piety among some who assume the role of issuing fatwas. He says: "Perhaps some of them permit this in situations where they claim necessity and urgent need based on the principle that necessities permit the prohibited, so they take what aligns with their interests until the issue arises in a situation where there is no necessity or need to adopt the weaker opinion or one that is outside the school of thought, and then they take the opinion that is established in the school or the stronger opinion in the school. This is also from that earlier category, as its essence is taking what aligns with the present desire, and the conditions of necessity are well known in the Sharia" (4). He then said: "The lack of piety and religion is evident in many who assume the role of disseminating knowledge and issuing fatwas, as previously exemplified. If this door were opened for them, the ties of the school would unravel, indeed all schools of thought, because what is obligatory for one thing is obligatory for its like, and it has become clear that the necessity claimed in the question is not a necessity" (5).
