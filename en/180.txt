The third category is that which is neither from the fundamentals (sulb) nor from the ancillary matters (milah), and it does not return to a definitive (qat'i) or speculative (dhanni) origin. Its nature is that it can be invalidated based on what is established as recognized knowledge and foundational principles referred to in actions and beliefs, or what leads to the invalidation of the truth and the establishment of falsehood. This is not knowledge because it returns to its origin through invalidation; it is neither stable nor authoritative nor consistent, and it is not from the ancillary matters, as the ancillary matters are those that are appreciated by minds and are pleasing to souls (1). He said about this category: "If it appeals to a people and they find it pleasing and seek it, it is due to a resemblance that has occurred and a confusion between it and what preceded it. Thus, the foolish may consider it as being based on an origin, and they may incline towards it from that perspective. The reality of its origin is an illusion and a delusion that has no truth, along with the ulterior motives and desires that accompany it" (2).
These texts from al-Shatibi indicate or establish his position regarding the methodology of analogy (ikhala) and suitability (munasaba), or how to consider public interest (maslaha), and the following research clarifies his stance on how to consider interests or wisdoms in legislation.
The meaning of returning to a definitive origin: Al-Shatibi stated that the foundational and relied-upon principle, which is the basis of demand, is what is definitive or returns to a definitive origin (3). In his first introduction, he said: "By general principles here, I mean the necessities, needs, and enhancements" (4). In his ninth introduction, he stated: "The necessities, needs, and enhancements, and what complements them and completes their aspects, are the foundations of the Sharia, and there is definitive proof regarding their consideration, with all branches relying on them" (5).
