Al-Shatibi's intention in this is that the legal texts must be understood based on their implications, as they were revealed to people for them to comprehend. They addressed them according to their existing knowledge and sciences, and if they had been addressed with something they were unfamiliar with or similar to it, they would not have understood it, and it would not have been a miracle for them. He states: "If it were not in accordance with what they were familiar with, it would not have been a miracle for them, and they would have dismissed it as not being in line with what we are accustomed to, since we have no experience with such speech" (1), just as if the Qur'an had been revealed in a foreign language.

And if the Arabs, to whom the Qur'an was revealed, had some knowledge, it is considered for the purpose of understanding the legal discourse, as they were addressed according to their characteristics, sciences, or knowledge. Therefore, if new sciences or meanings arise that they are unfamiliar with, they are not specifically intended in the address of the legislator, even if they are within the capacity of the words. The same applies if new meanings arise for words they used to employ. Thus, words are interpreted by the meanings that were in use at the time of their revelation. He says: "It is essential in understanding the Sharia to follow the customary understanding of the unlettered ones, namely the Arabs to whom the Qur'an was revealed in their language. If the Arabs have a consistent custom in their language, it is not correct to deviate from it in understanding the Sharia. If there is no custom, it is not correct to interpret it based on what is unfamiliar to them, and this applies to meanings, words, and styles" (2).
