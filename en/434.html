Since the general term means that it is established absolutely and universally based on beneficial induction that leads to certainty, this means here that the general is definitive, that it applies to all its particulars, and that it cannot be abrogated, nor can any part of it be abrogated. The universals cannot be abrogated, as previously demonstrated. Specification, in the sense of lifting the ruling from some particulars or in some cases, is considered a refutation of induction; therefore, there is no existence of specification in this sense. He says: "When a general or absolute principle is established, it is not affected by the cases of particulars or the narratives of situations, and the evidence for this consists of several points: the first is that the principle is definitively established by assumption, as we are discussing the definitive universal principles, while the cases of particulars are presumed or imagined, and the presumed does not stand against the definitive nor contradict it. The second is that the principle is not subject to doubt as it is based on definitive evidence, while the cases of particulars are subject to the possibility of being other than they appear or as they appear and are thus cut off and excluded from that principle; therefore, in this situation, it is not possible to invalidate the universality of the principle with something of this nature. The third is that the cases of particulars are specific, while the established principles are universals, and particulars cannot refute universals" (1). He then said: "The fourth is that if they oppose each other, either both must be applied together or both must be neglected, or one must be applied without the other, specifically in the area of opposition. Applying both together is invalid. Likewise, neglecting both is also invalid because it involves applying opposition between the presumed and the definitive, and applying the particular without the universal gives preference to the particular over the universal, which is contrary to the principle; thus, only the fourth option remains, which is to apply the universal without the particular, which is what is required" (2).
