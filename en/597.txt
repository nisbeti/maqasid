If an action is permissible in its essence, this means that it has a desired outcome which is intended by the legislator in enacting the action. If another intention is concomitant with this intended outcome, then this other outcome is either permitted or prohibited. If it is permitted, there is no issue; but if it is prohibited, this means that both a command and a prohibition converge on two concomitant outcomes, which is the subject here.
Al-Shatibi states that one of the intentions is subordinate while the other is the principal one, and the ruling is that of the principal one, with its subordinate being attached to it in ruling. This is similar to contracts concerning properties and their benefits; for instance, a contract for ownership of the land entails that the benefits of the land are subordinate to the ownership. Conversely, a contract for the benefits of the land means that the ownership is subordinate to the benefits in some manner, but not entirely. He said: "If the benefits are what is intended, then the ownership is subordinate as it is a means to the intended goal. If he meant that it is entirely subordinate to them, then that is prohibited" (1). He then said: "And if he meant some form of subordination, that is accepted and does not entail any prohibition, for general matters are related to their particulars in some way, and this does not necessitate their complete subordination to them" (2).
