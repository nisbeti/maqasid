Al-Shawkani said in his definition of reasoning: "In their terminology, it is that which is neither a text, nor consensus, nor analogy" (1). He then said: "They differed regarding its types; it was said that there are three: the connection between the two rulings without specifying a reason, otherwise it would be analogy. The second: the presumption of continuity (istishab). The third: the legislation from before us. The Hanafis stated this. Among its types, there is a fourth type which is juristic preference (istihsan). The Malikis stated that among its types there is a fifth type which is the public interest (maslaha) that is not specified" (2). He said: "The public interest... was mentioned by a group of scholars of usul (principles of jurisprudence) in the discussions of reasoning, and for this reason, some called it the unrestricted reasoning, and Imam al-Haramayn and Ibn al-Sam'ani referred to it as reasoning" (3). Al-Amidi said: "As for its meaning in language, it is a verbal noun derived from seeking evidence and the guiding path to the desired, and as for in the terminology of jurists, it is sometimes used to mean mentioning the evidence, whether the evidence is a text, consensus, analogy, or otherwise, and it is sometimes used to refer to a specific type of evidence... which is an evidence that is neither a text, nor consensus, nor analogy... and it has various types" (4). He then mentioned types that are all related to logic and the method of arranging premises and deriving conclusions (5).
Thus, reasoning is a type of evidence that is neither a text, nor consensus, nor analogy. What is meant here is the public interest, which resembles analogy and the method of suitability in that it connects a ruling to a suitable meaning, and it is not analogy and differs from suitability in that there is no original source that supports the meaning, but the meaning exists in many legal branches.
