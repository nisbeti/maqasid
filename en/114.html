Al-Razi said: "A suitable analogy that does not have a specific foundational evidence to support it, meaning that it considers its genus within its genus, but there is no foundational evidence indicating the consideration of its specific instance within its specific instance, and this is what is known as public interest (maslaha) that is not specified."(1) Then he said: "An example of the effect of the genus within the genus is the reasoning for rulings that do not have specific foundational evidence, such as when Ali (may Allah be pleased with him) considered drinking as equivalent to slander, treating the presumption of something as equivalent to its actual instance, analogous to treating seclusion with a woman as equivalent to having sexual intercourse with her in terms of prohibition."(2) This same example was mentioned by al-Shawkani as an example of a strange analogy.(3)

Thus, the mursal (transmitted without a chain of narration) according to those who accept it is either strange or suitable; it does not have a specific origin that testifies to it, nor has it ever had a witness to its origin. As for what is mursal in general, it is rejected without dispute.

The appropriate strange (ghareeb) is rejected by al-Shatibi. He does not suffice with the testimony of one origin or a few origins to affirm the consideration of the type, but rather he requires that many origins testify to it, to the extent that it reaches certainty in his view. He says: "It must be compatible with the actions of the Sharia, which means that there exists for that meaning a type that the legislator has considered in general without specific evidence, which is the general reasoning known as the unqualified public interest (maslaha mursala)"(4). He also says: "Every legal origin that does not have specific textual evidence and is compatible with the actions of the Sharia, and whose meaning is derived from its evidences, is valid and can be relied upon and referred to if that origin has become certain by the totality of its evidences... and this falls under the general reasoning that was adopted by Malik and al-Shafi'i"(5).
