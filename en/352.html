Al-Shatibi mentioned customs (adat) in the introduction of his book on the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) and categorized them as one of four sections: acts of worship (ibadat), customs, transactions (muamalat), and crimes (jina'at). He exemplified this with the consumption of food, drinks, clothing, and other consumables (1). However, in this research, he refers to them as one of two categories: acts of worship and customs. Therefore, it means here what is aside from acts of worship in terms of rulings. He states: "The essence of customs is the consideration of meanings" (2), and he supports this with several points, the first of which is induction. He says: "We found that the legislator aimed at the interests of the servants, and the ordinary rulings revolve with it wherever it goes. You see a single thing being prohibited in a situation where there is no interest, but if there is an interest, it is permissible, like the dirham for dirham for a deferred period, which is prohibited in sale but permissible in a loan... We did not find this in the realm of acts of worship as we understood it in customs. And Allah, Exalted is He, said: { And there is for you in retaliation the saving of life, O you who possess intellect } (3), and He said: { And do not consume one another's wealth unjustly } (4), and in the hadith: "A judge should not rule while angry" (5), and He said: "There should be neither harm nor reciprocating harm" (6), and He said: "The killer does not inherit" (7) (8). Then he said: "And so on, which cannot be enumerated, all of which indicates, and indeed states, the consideration of the interests of the servants, and that permission revolves with them wherever it goes, as clarified by the paths of reasoning. This indicates that customs are among those matters where the legislator relied on the consideration of meanings" (9).
