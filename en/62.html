The description being a defining characteristic of the existence of the ruling, meaning that the cause is indeed a cause, is a legal matter that requires evidence from the Sharia. Therefore, it has been defined as: "The evidence of the text indicates that it is a defining characteristic of the legal ruling."

The rationale (illa) has what the cause has; it is also a sign or indication that identifies the existence of the ruling. However, it is more than that, as it encompasses a rational meaning. Thus, the rationale is a cause, but not every cause is a rationale. An example of this is theft, which is an identifying sign of the existence of the ruling that mandates cutting off the hand, and adultery is an identifying sign of the existence of the ruling that mandates flogging or stoning. So, theft was a cause, and adultery was a cause, but the arrangement of the punishment for the act includes a wisdom that is suitable for reasoning with it. The description was not merely an abstract sign devoid of a rational meaning; it was not just a cause but rather a rationale(1).

(1) It should be noted here that theft, adultery, and other prescribed punishments, although they are rationales, cannot be used for analogy (qiyas) because the rationale here is not a description in the place to which the ruling was given, meaning it is not part of the place, but rather the whole place itself. If we want to extend the ruling of theft or adultery to another place (i.e., to a branch), it can only extend to theft or adultery. The ruling is then taken from the generality of the text and not by analogy. The incident that receives the same ruling is considered a root and not a branch. These rationales are called limited rationales because they do not extend to a branch. They are called rationales because they contain the meaning of a rationale.
