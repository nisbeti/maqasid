On this basis, a person is not tasked with what does not fall under their ability, including the traits they are naturally endowed with, such as hunger and thirst and similar matters; they are not required to eliminate these, but they are asked to control themselves from inclining towards what is unlawful. As for traits that are ambiguous in nature, such as love, hatred, anger, and others, they are to be examined; the request may either be directed towards their antecedents or their consequences. He states: "What is inherent in human nature in terms of traits inevitably leads to acquired actions, so the request is directed at those actions, not at what they originated from" (1). This is similar to love, where the request pertains to the causes, as in the saying of the Prophet (peace and blessings be upon him): "Exchange gifts, and you will love one another" (2). And regarding the desire for unlawful sexual relations, it is a consequence for which a person is required to eliminate its cause, which is the provocative gaze (3). As for anger, for example, a person is required to manage its consequences, such as refraining from revenge. He says: "Even if the provocation is not within their ability, the request pertains to the consequences, like anger provoking the desire for revenge, just as the gaze provokes the desire for unlawful relations" (4).
He concludes by saying: "If it is arranged in this manner, it is not correct to impose a duty regarding them themselves, even if it appears outwardly that such an imposition is evident; it is directed towards something other than that which precedes it, follows it, or coincides with it, and Allah knows best" (5).
Directing the implications of the request towards the consequences, antecedents, or accompanying factors, in accordance with this principle, necessitates the application of other principles to determine what the request pertains to, and the meaning to which it is directed cannot be considered valid except after establishing its meaning through induction.
