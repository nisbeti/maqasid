And this division of the objectives (maqasid) can be understood from two perspectives:

The first: The observable reality in humans is that they have needs, and these needs vary in life and differ in the degree of necessity. Some needs are indispensable for human survival, and their absence leads to death; others are essential for the community, and their absence leads to chaos and conflict; some lead to disturbance and hardship; and some are less critical than that, and so on. It is natural for a human, when legislating or trying to understand the philosophy of any legislation, to take into account what they perceive of these needs and the degree of necessity for them. They would formulate their legislation according to what they see as achieving the best outcomes based on their concepts, and it is natural for them to include in their considerations the trade-offs between interests and harms according to their understandings. Thus, a person may consider some needs as essential and others as luxury, and others as falling in between. All of this is based on what they perceive and find within themselves. People differ in this regard based on their concepts; some may see freedom of belief and freedom of opinion and the right to change one's religion as fundamental human rights and a necessary need, thus considering the right to apostasy as a necessity. Others may view this as one of the greatest evils, believing that the necessity is to kill the apostate after giving them a chance to repent if they do not return, as it is essential for preserving the faith. Some may see adultery as a corruption that undermines the fundamental objective of preserving lineage, while others view it as a personal freedom and an interest. Some see usury as a necessity for society and transactions, ranking it among the essentials, while others regard it as a need, and yet others consider it one of the greatest evils.
