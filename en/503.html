And this can only occur in the application of ready-made rulings to their occurrences and in analogy (qiyas). He says: "Thus, the jurists apply the legal rulings to verbal occurrences that are not in Arabic, and they consider the terms in many of the new issues. Furthermore, the analogical reasoning does not require the implications of the terms except in relation to the basis of comparison, which is the original case, and it may be accepted as a given or based on the stated reason or one that is alluded to. This is accepted as a given, while everything else refers back to rational consideration, and to this type belongs the ijtihad (juristic reasoning) attributed to the followers of the imams of ijtihad" (1). Ijtihad, according to him, is divided into ijtihad for deriving the reasons and general principles, ijtihad for branching them out and applying them to their occurrences, and a third ijtihad for determining their occurrences. The latter does not require knowledge of the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) or knowledge of Arabic. He says: "Ijtihad may relate to the determination of the subject matter, and it does not depend on knowledge of the objectives of the legislator, just as it does not depend on knowledge of the science of Arabic" (2). This is like investigating whether this is wine, which is prohibited, or a beverage, which is permissible, or whether this witness is just, so his testimony is accepted, or a transgressor, so it is not accepted, or whether this person is poor, deserving of charity, or not, so he is not given from it. This does not require knowledge of the Sharia or Arabic, but rather requires knowledge of the subject of the research. Therefore, the person must be knowledgeable in identifying wine, in understanding justice, and in recognizing poverty. If he needs legal information, he can accept it as a given.
