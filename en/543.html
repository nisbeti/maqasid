This principle, the principle of considering the outcomes of actions, is not a principle like other principles or rules in al-Shatibi's methodology; rather, it is the foundation of his approach and the essence of the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia). Most of the other principles and rules that he considers in ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning) and legislation are built upon it. The rules of blocking the means (sadd al-dhara'i), preference (istihsan), public interest (maslaha), removing harm (raf' al-darar), alleviating hardship (raf' al-haraj), and not imposing what is beyond one's capacity or burdensome difficulties, as well as the rule of legal tricks (hiyal) and others, are all based on this principle, since the objectives of the Sharia are its outcomes, and this principle pertains to the outcomes of actions, and the rulings are indeed rulings on actions.

His proofs:

Thus, we see that al-Shatibi, when he obligated the mujtahid (jurist) to consider the outcomes of actions and provided evidence for this, used the same proofs he employed to establish that the Sharia was enacted for the benefit of the servants. He cited three matters. He said: "The evidence for its validity consists of three matters. The first is that the obligations, as previously mentioned, are legislated for the interests of the servants, and the interests of the servants are either worldly or otherworldly. As for the otherworldly, it pertains to the outcome of the obligated person in the Hereafter" (1), and he said: "As for the worldly, if you contemplate actions, they are precedents for the results of interests, for they are causes for effects that are intended by the legislator, and the effects are the outcomes of the causes, so considering them in the flow of causes is necessary, which is the meaning of considering the outcomes" (2). As for his second proof, he stated that not considering the outcomes of actions contradicts the fact that obligations were legislated for the interests of the servants (3). He added: "Moreover, this leads to not seeking benefit through a permissible action nor expecting harm from a prohibited action, which is contrary to the purpose of the Sharia" (4). It is clear that these two proofs return to the core of his idea that the Sharia was enacted for the interests of the servants.
