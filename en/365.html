As for those who specified the intention (niyyah) with the particular legal meaning, they differentiated between acts of worship (ibadat) and customary actions (adat). The acts of customary actions are valid, even if they are not intended to comply with the Sharia, but they do not yield any reward. As for acts of worship, intention is a condition for their validity. Ibn Rajab al-Hanbali said: "There has been a disagreement regarding the interpretation of the saying: 'Actions are judged by intentions.' Many of the later scholars claim that its interpretation is: actions are valid or recognized or accepted based on intentions, and based on this, the actions refer specifically to the legal actions that require intention. However, actions that do not require intention, such as customary actions like eating, drinking, dressing, and others, or like returning trusts and guaranteed items, such as deposits and usurpations, do not require any intention, so all of this is excluded from the generality of the actions mentioned here. Others said: No, the actions here are in their generality, and nothing is excluded from them" (1).

The essence of this disagreement returns to consensus regarding acts of worship, as there is no disagreement that the validity of acts of worship depends on intention. As for customary actions, they are the subject of disagreement and detail, and those who limit the meaning of the hadith "Actions are judged by intentions" to acts of worship emphasize that the intentions of the doer affect customary actions. Therefore, they established a "legal" principle broader than the hadith on intention, based on the hadith and other legal texts, which is the principle: "Matters are judged by their intentions."
