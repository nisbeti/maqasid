Al-Suyuti said: "The basis of this principle is his saying - peace and blessings be upon him -: 'Actions are judged by intentions.' This is a well-known authentic hadith narrated by the six imams and others from Umar ibn al-Khattab"(1).
It is thus comprehensive in this text for both acts of worship and customary practices. Ibn Nujaym al-Hanafi said under the title: "Matters are judged by their intentions": "Selling juice from someone who makes it into wine is not prohibited if the intention is for trade, but it is prohibited if the intention is for fermentation; the same applies to planting a vineyard. Similarly, grape juice intended for vinegar or wine, and the act of boycotting for more than three days depends on the intention; if the intention is to boycott a Muslim, it is prohibited, otherwise it is not"(2), and the examples mentioned in the above text are not acts of worship.
Therefore, they said that intention was legislated to distinguish acts of worship from customary practices and to differentiate the ranks of acts of worship from one another; as for customary practices, intention is not a condition for them, but rather the condition is that the intention must be lawful. This is in addition to the condition that the act itself must be lawful.
Ibn al-Sabki said: "It also includes(3) the principle: intention to distinguish acts of worship from customary practices and to differentiate the ranks of acts of worship from one another. The first is like sitting in the mosque, wavering between sitting for i'tikaf and sitting for rest, and like ablution and ghusl, wavering between cooling off and cleaning, and seeking closeness; without intention, the act of worship would not be distinguished from others, nor would it be valid"(4). He said: "As for the second, which is their saying: to distinguish the ranks of acts of worship from one another, it is like distinguishing voluntary prayer from obligatory prayer"(5).
