Al-Suyuti said: "The basis of this principle is his saying - peace and blessings be upon him -: 'Actions are judged by intentions.' This is a well-known authentic hadith reported by the six imams and others from Umar ibn al-Khattab" (1).
It is thus comprehensive of both acts of worship and customary practices. Ibn Najim al-Hanafi stated under the title: "Matters are judged by their intentions": "Selling juice by someone who makes it into wine is not prohibited if the intention is for trade, but if the intention is for fermentation, it is prohibited. Likewise, planting a vineyard follows this rule. And similarly, grape juice intended for vinegar or wine, and the act of boycotting for more than three days depends on the intention; if the intention is to boycott a Muslim, it is prohibited, otherwise it is not" (2), and the examples mentioned in the above text are not acts of worship.
Therefore, they said that intention was legislated to distinguish acts of worship from customary practices and to differentiate the ranks of acts of worship from one another; as for customary practices, intention is not a condition for them, but rather the condition is that the intention must be lawful. This is in addition to the requirement that the action itself is lawful.
Ibn al-Sabki said: "It also includes the principle: intention is for distinguishing acts of worship from customary practices and for differentiating the ranks of acts of worship from one another. The first is like sitting in the mosque, which varies between sitting for i'tikaf (spiritual retreat) and sitting for rest, and like ablution and bathing, which vary between cooling off, cleaning, and seeking closeness to Allah; without intention, the act of worship would not be distinguished from others, nor would it be valid" (3). He said: "As for the second, which is their saying: 'to distinguish the ranks of acts of worship from one another,' it is like distinguishing voluntary prayer from obligatory prayer" (4).
