The Sharia (Islamic law) was established to achieve the objectives of the Lawgiver (al-Shari) in creation. Therefore, it is necessary to understand the Sharia and the objectives of the Lawgiver. For this reason, the Lawgiver addressed people in a manner they could comprehend, speaking to them in Arabic, using Arabic words, structures, and styles. He obligated them to understand the reports and rulings according to the implications of the texts as understood by the Arabs. Al-Shatibi says: "This blessed Sharia is Arabic, and there is no place for foreign tongues in it" (1). Al-Shatibi does not mean by this that the Qur'an does not contain foreign words; this has been addressed by the scholars of usul (principles of jurisprudence). Rather, his point is that "it was revealed in the language of the Arabs, with its specific words and styles of meaning, and that it speaks in accordance with what it is naturally inclined to in its language, addressing the general with the intended meaning of its apparent sense, and the general intended in one aspect and the specific in another, and the general intended as specific, and the apparent intended as non-apparent. All of this is understood from the beginning, middle, or end of the speech, and it speaks in a way that its beginning indicates its end or its end indicates its beginning" (2). He then said: "It is not possible to understand the language of the Arabs from the perspective of understanding the language of the non-Arabs due to the differences in structures and styles" (3).

(3) Al-Shatibi, al-Muwafaqat, 2/44. Al-Shafi'i mentioned this matter in several places in his book (al-Risala), perhaps most notably on pages 51 - 52 - 53. Among what he said: "Indeed, Allah addressed the Arabs in His Book in their language according to what they know of its meanings, and among what they know of its meanings is the expansiveness of their language, and that its nature is to address something in a general apparent sense intended as the general apparent, and the beginning of this suffices for its end, and a general apparent intended as general that includes the specific, and this is indicated by some of what they were addressed with therein. And a general apparent intended as specific, and the apparent is known in its context to be intended as non-apparent. All of this is known from the beginning, middle, or end of the speech. And it begins a matter in its speech that clarifies its first word in it from its last, and it begins a matter that clarifies its last word in it from its first... and it speaks of something that it knows by meaning without clarification by word, just as it defines by indication, and this is considered among its highest speech due to the exclusivity of its scholars over those who are ignorant of it" (See: al-Risala, paragraphs 175, 174, 173). Among what al-Shafi'i said: "The language of the Arabs is the most expansive of languages in its methodology and has the most words, and we do not know of anyone other than a prophet who can encompass all its words, but nothing escapes it from its generality, so that there is no one among them who does not know it" (al-Risala, p. 42, paragraph 139). In (al-Risala), there are sections with the following titles: Clarification of what was revealed from the Book as general intended as general that includes the specific, clarification of what was revealed from the Book as general apparent that encompasses both general and specific, clarification of what was revealed from the Book as general apparent intended as entirely specific. See, pp. 53 - 62.
