As for the consideration of interests, which he called the subsidiary objectives (maqasid tab'iyya), this means that the legal rulings pertain to the obligated person in terms of preservation, security, care, and obtaining what is necessitated by their sensual attributes. Induction has indicated this, so the attainment of these interests has become a legal objective, and it is permissible for the obligated person to intend through their actions the fulfillment of these needs and the attainment of these desires, and for their actions to be aimed at acquiring these interests. However, he still referred to them as subsidiary objectives in contrast to the original objectives, meaning that it is permissible for him to intend them, but only within the limits of what is legally permissible; that is, it is not permissible for him to pursue them except through actions that are legally authorized. In this case, the intention of interests strengthens the idea of worship, and this was previously mentioned in his seventh introduction where he said: "Every legal knowledge is sought by the legislator only as a means to worship Allah, Exalted is He, and not for any other reason. If another consideration appears in it, it is only secondary and by way of the second intention, not the first" (1), and he mentioned in this introduction his proofs, including: "The law was only revealed for the purpose of worship, which is the objective of the sending of the prophets, peace be upon them" (2). He said: "Every subsidiary objective is either a servant of the original intention or not. If it serves it, then the intention towards it from the outset is correct... But if it does not serve it, then the intention towards it from the outset is incorrect" (3).
... Thus, the subsidiary objectives are legal objectives that the obligated person also intends because they align with their nature and attributes, but it is not permissible to intend them if that is through violating the legal rulings or texts. He said regarding those who take their interests without adhering to the commands and prohibitions: "As a precaution against those who take it without considering the command and prohibition, and this is the blameworthy interest, for they do not refrain from what has been prescribed for them but rather act boldly like an animal that knows nothing but walking in its desires" (4).
