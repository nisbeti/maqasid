The public interest (maslaha) according to al-Shatibi is what has been established as being intended by the legislator through the ruling. The harm (mafsada) is what has been established as being intended by the legislator through the ruling when it does not occur. This is known through the examination of texts and rulings. If it is established that an action is commanded, then the action is a public interest; if it is established that it is prohibited, then it is a harm. The same applies to the results, outcomes, or causes of the action. What is established through examination to be the reason for the ruling of the laws is a public interest, as actions are causes for outcomes, as are the rulings of actions. What is established through examination to be the reason for the prohibition of the laws is a harm. This is confirmed by looking at the causal relationship between the ruling and its results and implications. The public interest, according to him, is not merely what brings benefit or what makes the obligated person feel aligned with human inclinations and needs, nor is it a condition for the public interest to be a suitable description. The same applies to the harm; rather, there must be a causal relationship between the ruling and what it is connected to. This meaning is extensively discussed in many places in his book, particularly in his discussions on causes and effects in the Book of Rulings, as well as in his discussions on the fourth type of objectives of the legislator. He said: "Prohibited causes are causes of harms, not of public interests, just as permissible causes are causes of public interests, not of harms" (1). He also said: "If that is the case, it is not correct for anyone to claim that the Sharia was established according to the desires and objectives of the people" (2). He stated: "The fact that the public interest is a public interest intended by the ruling and the harm is a harm likewise is something specific to the legislator, and there is no room for reason in this based on the principle of negation of good and evil. If the legislator has established a ruling for a certain public interest, then he is the one who has established it as a public interest; otherwise, it could logically be that it is not so, as all things are equal in relation to their original status, and reason has no jurisdiction over them in terms of goodness or badness."
