The derivation of legal rulings requires the application of both particulars and universals together, and although the universals are established through the induction of particulars, it is not correct to apply the universal while neglecting the particular. For example: "If it is known that the preservation of necessities is considered, but knowledge of the specific means of preservation has not been attained, then there are various ways of preservation that the intellect may grasp or may not grasp, and if it does grasp them, it may do so in relation to one condition rather than another or one time rather than another" (1). An example of this is that knowledge of the principle of preserving wealth, for instance, is not sufficient to clarify the ruling on whether the thief should be imprisoned, executed, have a hand cut off, or something else, all of which are consistent with the principle, and it is not sufficient to distinguish between the deceiver, the usurper, and the thief in terms of ruling. Knowledge of particulars requires knowledge of universals for the validity of derivation, as particulars can have multiple meanings, and the correct meaning is the one that refers back to a universal, and if it contradicts a universal, it has contradicted a definitive one, which is not valid, and the negation of universals is also a negation of particulars.
