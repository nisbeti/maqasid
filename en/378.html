And the principle: "Actions are judged by their intentions" is acknowledged by al-Shatibi in the same sense mentioned by the jurists in the previous discussion, even though he does not state it explicitly. He said: "Indeed, actions are based on intentions, and the objectives are considered in transactions of both acts of worship and customary practices, and the evidence for this meaning is not limited; it suffices for you that the objectives differentiate between what is customary and what is an act of worship, and in acts of worship, between what is obligatory and what is non-obligatory, and in customary practices, between the obligatory, the recommended, the permissible, the disliked, the prohibited, the valid, the invalid, and other rulings. A single action may be intended for one purpose and thus be an act of worship, while if intended for another purpose, it would not be so; rather, it may be intended for one thing and thus be an act of faith, and intended for another thing and thus be disbelief, such as prostrating to Allah or to an idol. Furthermore, when an action is associated with intention, it is connected to the legal rulings, and if it is devoid of intention, none of those rulings apply to it, such as the actions of a sleeping person, an inattentive person, or a madman"(1). He also said: "Indeed, all actions that fall under choice do not become acts of worship except with the intention for that. As for what is established as an act of worship, such as prayer and pilgrimage, there is no doubt about it, while customary practices do not become acts of worship except through intentions"(2).

The lawful actions are either intended by the obligated person solely for compliance, which are the primary objectives, or they are intended solely for their own interests, which are the secondary objectives. Alternatively, they may intend both purposes together.
