In both cases, the burdensome hardship (al-mashaqa al-mu'ntah) is the reason established by Islamic law for the concession (rukhsah), but since hardships are relative, meaning they differ among individuals and cannot be standardized, the legislator has set specific reasons (causes) to identify them. Therefore, if the reason exists, it is permissible to act upon the concession even if the burdensome hardship does not exist, because the concession is explicitly stated. If the reason exists along with the burdensome hardship, such as when there is a significant hardship during travel, it becomes obligatory to relieve the hardship, similar to how a person in dire need must eat something unlawful, for example. It is called a concession because it falls under the definition of a concession.

He says: "The true hardship is the reason established for the concession; if it does not exist, the ruling is not obligatory, unless the presumption arises, which is the reason, taking the place of the wisdom, in which case the reason becomes sufficient for permissibility rather than obligation" (1). He also says: "There is no specific criterion or fixed limit for the considered hardship in alleviations that applies to all people; therefore, the law has established the reason in many cases to take the place of the cause because it is the closest presumption of the existence of hardship, leaving each individual to what they find... and leaving much of it to ijtihad (independent reasoning)" (2). Let us pay attention to his statement: "in many cases," meaning the explicitly stated concessions. This indicates that some of them do not have a reason established by the law that takes the place of the cause, and thus we apply the cause itself for alleviation, which is the true hardship. Here, the concession in the second sense comes into play, which is in the presence of the true hardship, meaning the burdensome one. His statement: "and leaving each individual to what they find..." indicates that some actions may be difficult for some and easy for others; therefore, these concessions are relative just as the hardships are relative. He says: "The concession is relative, not original, meaning that everyone is an expert in taking it for themselves unless a legal limit is set that they must adhere to" (3). He said: "There may be a man who is accustomed to cutting through difficulties until it becomes a habit for him, and he does not feel burdened or distressed by it... and another man is the opposite" (4). He said: "Many people can endure in their illness what others cannot, so the concession is legislated for one of the two men but not the other, and there is no doubt about this" (5).
