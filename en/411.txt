It has been mentioned in the introduction to this chapter that this discussion addresses the rules related to sources of legislation in general. That is, it pertains to all of them, and these rules are divided into two categories: the first relates to how to implement the legal texts from the Qur'an and the Sunnah, meaning how to apply the meanings of the texts to events and actions, and the same applies to how to implement the implications of analogy (qiyas) and what has been agreed upon. The second relates to the subsequent attributes of the texts in terms of definiteness and similarity, abrogation (naskh), generality and specification, commands and prohibitions, general and specific, and unrestricted and restricted. The first pertains to how to derive meanings, which are both general and specific meanings. The second pertains to how to determine these meanings, as it is influenced by similarity, ambiguity, abrogation, specification, or restriction.
It should be noted that the discussion of these two categories here is only in relation to al-Shatibi's methodology specifically, that is, in terms of its novelty as a methodological approach.
Al-Shatibi's perspective on the implications of legal texts:
Al-Shatibi believes that the implication of individual texts on meanings is a probabilistic implication. He denies the existence of a definitive implication of the text on the meaning. As for the implication of rulings on their objectives or purposes, al-Shatibi completely denies considering them if reliance is placed on individual rulings.
His denial of definitive implication is indicated by his statement: "The relied-upon in the primary intention is the legal evidence, and the existence of definiteness in it based on the commonly known usage is nonexistent, or extremely rare, I mean in individual evidences. If they are from the reports of individuals, then their failure to provide definiteness is evident. And if they are mutawatir (consecutively reported), then their provision of definiteness is contingent upon premises, all or most of which are probabilistic. What is contingent upon the probabilistic must be probabilistic, as it depends on the transmission of languages, grammatical opinions, the absence of ambiguity, the absence of metaphor, legal or customary transmission, implicitness, specification of the general, restriction of the unrestricted, the absence of abrogation, precedence and postponement, and rational opposition. The provision of definiteness while considering these matters is impossible." (1)
