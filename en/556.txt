The principles of religion, like the beliefs, are definitive and cannot be contradicted by anything. Likewise, the three categories—necessities (darurat), needs (hajiyat), and enhancements (tahsinat)—are understood from the Sharia. The general principle is not invalidated by the opposition of some particulars to it. His statement: "It is necessary to understand them correctly, for they are the basis of disagreement and contention," means that these established principles, which are derived through induction, are contested by some who reject them based on particular cases that contradict them. Therefore, it is essential to understand correctly that particulars do not invalidate the principles. What he means is that there are those who dispute and oppose the validity of the principle of considering the consequences of actions (mala'at al-af'al), based on what has been mentioned in the texts regarding these rejected consequences, which have not affected rulings such as marriage and the pursuit of knowledge, nor have they changed the ruling. Thus, they claim that this principle has fallen. This claim is invalid because the principle is a definitive generality, while what they assert are particular cases and specific issues that do not reach the level of being definitive. His statement: "What is transmitted from the righteous predecessors that contradicts this are specific cases that have no evidence in themselves until their meaning is understood, and they align with what has been established, if Allah wills," means that what the disputants raise in refuting these principles, including the principle of considering consequences, has no evidence, as it is based on particulars. If these particulars converge on a specific meaning and this meaning is subjected to an induction that yields certainty, then it will become clear that this certain meaning and these particulars related to it will align with the principle of considering consequences. This is because the principles are definitive generalities and do not contradict each other, and since they are particulars that have not yet reached this level, they remain speculative in their opposition to the definitive principle and are subject to interpretation.

(1) Necessities (darurat) refer to fundamental needs that are essential for the preservation of religion, life, intellect, lineage, and property.
(2) Needs (hajiyat) are those things that are not essential but are necessary for ease and comfort in life.
(3) Enhancements (tahsinat) refer to those aspects that improve the quality of life but are not strictly necessary.
(4) The principle of considering the consequences of actions (mala'at al-af'al) is a key concept in Islamic jurisprudence that emphasizes the importance of the outcomes of actions in determining their permissibility or prohibition.
