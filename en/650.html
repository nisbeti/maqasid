Both induction and deduction are rational methodologies of knowledge, meaning they are ways of thinking aimed at reaching conclusions. Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr said: "The reasoning that human thought typically engages in is divided into two main categories: one is deduction, and the other is induction. Each of the deductive and inductive proofs has its own methodology and distinctive path. By deduction, we mean any reasoning whose conclusion is not greater than the premises from which that reasoning is derived" (1). He also said: "By induction, we mean any reasoning in which the conclusion is greater than the premises that contributed to that reasoning. For example, it is said: this piece of iron expands with heat, and that one expands with heat, and this third piece also expands with heat; therefore, all iron expands with heat" (2). He stated: "In cases of induction, the inductive proof jumps from the specific to the general" (3).

What all Muslims unanimously agree upon is that Islam has come with legal texts from which legal meanings are derived through deduction, including rulings. The rulings are general concerning the obligated individuals and actions, and they are universal through the causes that transfer them from one type of action to another. These causes are deduced from the legal proofs. When a text is presented, the meaning is taken from it, and the text or its indication is not considered a part of the meaning, nor is it a phenomenon that must be repeated in many texts and indications to establish the general meaning. This repetition is the consistency that benefits from induction, as al-Shatibi mentioned, and rulings are not derived in this manner; rather, they are derived from the indications of the texts, that is, from the detailed proofs. Therefore, jurisprudence (fiqh) is defined as the knowledge of practical legal rulings derived from their detailed proofs.
