... These meanings, although they are not from the indications of the texts, serve as foundational principles or definitive reasons upon which the rulings are established. This means that these rulings are issued based on the application of those principles. Thus, these rulings are particulars in relation to them, and the meaning of the principles is embodied within them. This is the meaning of the descent of the universals to the particulars, and therefore attention must be paid to the meanings, that is, to a specific meaning or meanings present in the ruling, and then to follow this meaning to achieve its induction, which then becomes a universal principle and a reason for public interest (maslaha). For example, in the hadith "the killer does not inherit," one can imagine the meaning of treating the opposite of the intended purpose as a punishment, or that whoever hastens a matter before its time is punished by being deprived of it. So, are these meanings principles or rules in the Sharia upon which the rulings are based? These meanings are not fixed as principles of the Sharia, even though they can be imagined in the ruling, and therefore it is necessary to trace this meaning and observe its presence or absence in many other rulings, and to note its occurrence or non-occurrence where it can be applied, and thus the induction that confirms or negates the meaning is achieved. This attention to meanings must occur in customary practices based on the principle that the Sharia was revealed for the interests of the servants, and these interests are the principles upon which the Sharia was established, and these interests must be present in the rulings. However, since the rulings can be imagined to contain many meanings or rulings, and it cannot be determined that this meaning is what the Legislator intended or upon which the ruling was established, it is necessary to conduct an induction for this meaning, because with the abundance of rulings, the meaning becomes stronger, and by being beyond limitation, it becomes definitive and intended by the Legislator and serves a public interest. He says: "The Legislator has expanded in explaining the reasons and rulings in legislating the domain of customs, as previously illustrated, and most of the reasons mentioned therein are based on suitability, which, when presented to the intellects, are accepted. Thus, we understand from this that the Legislator intended to follow the meanings rather than to rely solely on the texts, unlike the domain of worship, where the known is the opposite of that" (1).
