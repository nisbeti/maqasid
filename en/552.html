It is legally permissible, as taking on beneficial actions is correct on the condition of exercising caution to the best of one's ability without hardship (1). This means that the required or permitted actions that fall within these categories, even if they lead to harmful outcomes, do not change their rulings, and it is permissible to undertake them despite the harms involved; neither do their rulings change, nor do the harms become benefits. In other words, prohibitions do not become permissible, but one should engage in what is lawful while avoiding what is forbidden as much as possible. If the avoidance reaches a point of hardship or harm or exceeds one's capacity, then the ruling may change based on legal principles, such as the principle that necessity permits the prohibited, or that one is not obliged to do what is beyond their ability, or due to the lifting of harm, or similar considerations. Al-Shatibi provides an example, stating: "Like marriage, which necessitates seeking sustenance for dependents amidst the narrow paths of what is lawful and the vast avenues of what is unlawful and questionable. Often, this compels one to engage in earning for them through means that are not permissible, yet this does not prevent the avoidance of the harm that arises from the expectation of facing the harm of exposure. If such considerations were applied to marriage in our time, it would lead to the invalidation of its essence, which is incorrect. Similarly, seeking knowledge if it involves witnessing immoral acts that one hears and sees, and attending funerals and performing religious duties if one can only do so by witnessing what is not acceptable, does not remove these occurrences from their foundational principles because they are the foundations of religion and the principles of benefits" (2).
