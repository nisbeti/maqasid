The meaning of this text is that the legal ruling (hukm) regarding the actions of a responsible person (mukallaf) when it comes from the legislator (al-shari) is such that the mukallaf should not consider the outcomes (ma'alat) or the benefits or harms that may result from it. This is because the legislator has already taken these into account in the ruling and has judged the mukallaf considering his condition and outcome, along with everything that must be considered. As for the jurist (mujtahid), when he exerts effort to understand the ruling, he must take into account the action and the outcome of the action because, in this regard, he is in the position of the legislator. He, the mujtahid, is responsible when he is making his ijtihad; he is responsible in considering the action and its outcome. Therefore, it is not his place to assume the role of the legislator and change the ruling based on the outcome of the action. Rather, his duty or obligation is to know the ruling of the legislator according to both the action and the outcome together. An example of this is the ruling on carrion (mayta), which is prohibited, but in some cases, such as extreme hunger (khumsa), abstaining from it may lead to the loss of life or to neglecting a necessity (darura). In this case, the ruling changes based on the change in the outcome. The mujtahid did not change the ruling based on benefit or outcome; rather, he derived and understood the ruling of the Sharia. The change in the ruling here is due to a change in the cause (sabab), just as it changes with the change in the action or the basis (manat). Similarly, fasting in Ramadan is obligatory, but in some cases, such as during jihad or for someone afflicted with a certain illness, fasting may lead to neglecting a more important obligation or to compromising a necessity, thus changing the ruling based on what it leads to. The application of this principle, the principle of the outcomes of actions, does not grant the mukallaf or the mujtahid the right to change rulings based on whims. Rather, the ruling changes only where the Sharia indicates that it should. If there is no evidence for changing the ruling, even if it leads to harm, then the ruling does not change. Likewise, the harm does not become permissible or excusable; rather, one should strive to avoid the harm as much as possible. If the Sharia does not indicate a change in the ruling, it does not change, even if it has an outcome contrary to what was intended. Just as the outcome is intended, the ruling itself is intended.
