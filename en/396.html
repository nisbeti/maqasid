Al-Shatibi said in an additional explanation regarding legal stratagems for the purpose of examining the ruling of Sharia on them: "Before considering their validity or lack thereof, it is necessary to explain this stratagem. Allah, Exalted is He, has mandated certain things and prohibited others, either absolutely without any condition or arrangement based on a cause, such as the obligation of prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, and similar acts, and as He has prohibited adultery, usury, murder, and the like. He has also mandated certain things contingent upon causes. Likewise, He has prohibited others, such as the obligation of zakat, expiations, fulfilling vows, and the right of preemption for a partner, as well as the prohibition of the divorced woman and the benefit from what is usurped or stolen, and similar matters. If a person responsible for fulfilling these obligations causes the removal of that obligation from himself or makes that which is prohibited permissible through some means of causation, so that the obligation appears not to be obligatory or the prohibition appears to be lawful, this causation is called a stratagem (hila) or legal stratagem (tahhil). For instance, if the time for prayer arrives while he is present, and it becomes obligatory for him to pray four units, he may seek to cause the removal of all of them by drinking alcohol or taking a mind-altering medicine until the time passes and he is devoid of his senses, like one who has fainted. Or he may shorten the prayer by initiating a journey to shorten it. Similarly, if someone is overshadowed by the month of Ramadan, he may travel to eat, or if he has wealth sufficient for pilgrimage, he may gift it away or destroy it in some manner to avoid the obligation of pilgrimage. Likewise, if he desires to have intercourse with another's slave girl, he may seize her and claim she has died, then be compensated for her value and have intercourse with her that way, or he may present false witnesses for the marriage of a virgin with her consent, and the judge rules accordingly. Or if he wishes to sell ten dirhams for twenty on credit, he may set the ten dirhams as the price for a garment and then sell the garment back to the original seller for twenty on credit. Or if he intends to kill someone, he may set a prepared trap for him, such as setting a spear or digging a well, and similarly, he may evade the obligation of zakat by gifting away the wealth or destroying it, or by gathering what is scattered or dispersing what is gathered, and so on with other examples of making the unlawful permissible and removing the obligatory."(1)
