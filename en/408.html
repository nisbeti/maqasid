Based on the methodology of al-Shatibi, which holds that all obligations are for the benefit of the servants in this world or the Hereafter, every obligation contains a right for the servants either immediately or eventually. He said: "Just as every legal ruling contains a right for the servants either immediately or eventually, based on the premise that the Sharia was established for the interests of the servants" (1). As for what pertains to the servant from worldly benefits, it falls under the servant's right. And what pertains to him in the Hereafter is the right of Allah. He said: "The right of the servant is what pertains to his interests in this world; if it is from the interests of the Hereafter, it is included among what is referred to as the right of Allah" (2). The right of the servant or his shares that return to him due to the right of Allah are what are called the subsidiary objectives (maqasid al-tabi'a), and it is permissible for the servant to waive his right, such as waiving his right to inheritance or to the spoils of jihad or to blood money (diya) or retribution (qisas), or for a woman to waive her right to maintenance and similar matters.

Based on the above, actions concerning the right of Allah and the right of the servant fall into three categories. Al-Shatibi says: "Actions concerning the right of Allah and the right of the human being are three categories: the first is what is purely the right of Allah, such as acts of worship, and its essence is devotion as previously mentioned; if the action conforms to the command, it is valid, otherwise it is not" (3). He then says: "The second is what includes both the right of Allah and the right of the servant, with the right of Allah being predominant, and its ruling reverts to the first" (4), because if the right of the servant is rendered legally insignificant, it is like that which is not considered; for if it were considered, it would be the one that is considered, and the assumption is the opposite, such as in the case of killing oneself, as the servant has no choice in surrendering himself to be killed without necessity, such as during trials and the like" (5). He then says: "The third is what both rights share, with the right of the servant being predominant" (6).
