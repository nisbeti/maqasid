In these previous texts, there are two matters: the first is that detailed evidences provide a presumption, and there is conclusive evidence that it is obligatory to act upon this presumption; the second is that presumption is acted upon in legal matters because it is based on conclusive evidence that has established, with certainty, the obligation to rely on it as a source or reference, and not every presumption is legal, but only that which is based on conclusive legal evidences. These two matters, along with the texts of the aforementioned imams, indicate al-Shatibi's deviation from the established methodology, and the error in what al-Shatibi has claimed, as these texts contain evidence for the obligation to act upon the presumptive without requiring that the presumptive meaning returns to a conclusive meaning; rather, the condition is to return to a conclusive source. If we add to this that al-Shatibi did not provide evidence for what he stated, nor did he clarify any errors in the statements of the imams, then this condition he imposed is invalid, especially since he claims that this is what the verses and reports have established and what the righteous predecessors acted upon. The inclusion of principles in beliefs is valid if the principles are those that do not depend on anything else; if they are dependent on something else, then they are branches of others, even if they are principles from which others branch out. In this case, if they are presumptive, there is no harm in that if there is conclusive evidence for the obligation to act upon them.

The obligation to act upon the presumptive:

What further confirms the error in al-Shatibi's view regarding the principles for which he required conclusive evidence, and the mistake he built upon that, is that the legal evidence must either be conclusive or refer back to something conclusive. The conclusive evidences for the obligation to act upon the presumptive, if its source is conclusive, do not require that its meaning returns to a conclusive meaning.
