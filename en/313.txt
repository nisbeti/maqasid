Al-Shatibi emphasizes the principle of compliance and its existence in every action of the servants, and this principle is not contradicted by the fact that the purpose of the Sharia is to preserve the interests of the servants, nor by the fact that the rulings take into account human nature, desires, and sensual characteristics. This objective is an end and a result, that is, a consequence of the causes, and the causes are compliance. The Sharia is established based on the requirement of worship and compliance, { And I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me } (51:56), and not based on the desires and aims of the servants. This is what the types of legal rulings necessitate. He says: "No one has the right to claim that the Sharia was established based on the desires and aims of the servants, as the rulings of the Sharia do not escape the five categories. As for obligation and prohibition, their contradiction to the requirement of indulgence that falls under choice is evident, as it is said to him: Do such and such whether you have an aim in it or not, and do not do such and such whether you have an aim in it or not. If the obligated person happens to have a compatible aim and a desire that motivates him according to the command or prohibition, it is by accident, not by origin. As for the other categories, although their apparent nature falls under the choice of the obligated person, they have entered by the legislator's inclusion of them under his choice, thus they revert to being removed from his choice" (2). Then he says: "Thus, the permissibility of the permissible, for example, does not necessitate its absolute inclusion under the choice of the obligated person as it is a decree from the legislator. At that point, his choice would be dependent on the legislator's stipulation, and his aim would be derived from the legal permission, not from natural indulgence. This is precisely what removes the obligated person from the impulse of his desires so that he becomes a servant of Allah" (3) (4).

(4) In these statements, there is a denial of the Western idea of public freedoms in Islam. This idea stems from the notion of separating religion from life... and that man is free and not bound by any law or system, except what he chooses for himself. Hence, they spoke of the rule of the people, which led to the idea of democracy in Western systems. The concept of public freedoms is different from the meaning of freedom that opposes slavery, and it is a concept of disbelief because it implies that there is no rule of Allah, nor legislation except for the people. Similarly, the concept of permissibility (ibaha) in Islam is not a matter of freedom but is based on the idea of servitude to Allah, who alone has the right to command, prohibit, permit, and prevent. Thus, permissibility is a choice from the legislator, and the servant is bound to accept this choice, and he is not free; he cannot prohibit what is permissible nor obligate it. Therefore, the claim of the idea of public freedoms in Islam is a mistaken claim that contradicts the legal principles, and the greater errors are what come in the details of this idea. For example, Sheikh Rashid Ghannouchi in his book (Public Freedoms in the Islamic State) argued that the purpose of preserving the intellect is one of the necessities in the Sharia, meaning freedom of belief and freedom of opinion, even though these freedoms contradict general and specific principles in Islam that are not limited to the preservation of religion or the obligation to rule by what Allah has revealed and the obligation to kill the apostate, among other things. See: al-Muwafaqat, edited by Abu Ubaida, in his introduction to the book, vol. 1, footnote, pp. 42-53. Also see: articles titled (Integrating Islamic Communities into Western Societies) by Mahmoud Abdul Karim Hassan, Al-Wa'i magazine, issues: 115, 116, 117, 118, for the year 1417 AH, 1997 CE.
