These ideas are the pillars of al-Shatibi's (may Allah be pleased with him) concept. Through discussing them, the correctness or incorrectness of some of his ideas becomes evident, such as the meaning of returning to the definitive (qat'i) in his view, and that the legal evidence is only accepted if it is definitive or refers back to something definitive. As for the next section, it will be - if Allah wills - a discussion of the foundation of the idea, which is the principle of reasoning behind the actions of Allah (Exalted is He), and to discuss a necessary consequence of his methodology, which is the possibility for the jurist (mujtahid) who is well-versed in the general principles of the Sharia to dispense with examining its particulars. This includes the non-requirement of knowledge of Arabic for the jurist who is knowledgeable about the objectives of the Sharia in general and in detail.

The requirement of certainty in the principles of jurisprudence:

Al-Shatibi held that the principles of jurisprudence (usul al-fiqh) must be definitive (qat'i) and that they are associated in this regard with beliefs (aqa'id). He clarified that his understanding of the principles differs from that of other scholars of usul. His understanding of the principles refers to the definitive meanings that jurisprudence relies upon or derives from.

Yes, he differs in this from the imams of usul (principles) and the jurists, and this is one of his novelties. This is something I see as a mistake. The seriousness of this mistake is magnified because it is in the principles, and it is not merely a principle among the principles, but rather a principle that governs the principles, indeed it governs the entire methodology.

The error in it is not in its being new, nor in its contradicting the methodology established by the imams, but rather in its subject matter, that is, in the meaning of this principle.
