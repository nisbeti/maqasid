Al-Shatibi divides the similarity in texts into two categories: real and additional. There is also a third category that does not pertain to the texts but rather to the legal basis (manat). He says: "The similarity that occurs in the Sharia is of two types: one is real and the other is additional, and this pertains to the Sharia itself. There is another type that relates to the legal basis upon which the rulings are applied; the first is what is meant by the verse (1), and its meaning refers to the fact that no way has been made for us to understand its meaning, nor has any evidence been established for what is meant by it" (2). He then said regarding the second type, which is additional: "Its explanation has been established in reality, but the observer has fallen short in their ijtihad (independent reasoning) or has deviated from the path of clarification following their desires, so it is incorrect to attribute the confusion to the evidences; rather, it should be attributed to the observers' shortcomings or ignorance" (3). As for the third category, he said: "The third type of similarity does not return to the evidences but rather to the legal basis of the evidences. The prohibition of eating carrion is clear, and the permission to eat the slaughtered animal is likewise clear. If carrion mixes with the slaughtered animal, confusion arises regarding what is eaten, not regarding the evidence for its permissibility or prohibition" (4).

Similarity does not occur in the general principles (kulliyat) because these are definitive meanings (qat'iyyat) (5).

Examples of real similarity include: "such as the issues of the Ascension (istawa), descent (nuzul), laughter (dhahik), hand (yad), foot (qadam), and similar matters" (6).
