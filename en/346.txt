"The second: what is known or presumed to be a cause that was not legislated for its sake from the outset. The evidence indicates that this causation is incorrect because the cause was not originally legislated for this presumed effect. If it was not legislated for it, then it does not result in its wisdom of bringing about a benefit or averting a harm concerning what was intended by the cause; thus, it is invalid. This is one aspect, and a second aspect is that this cause, regarding this presumed objective, is not legislated, so it becomes like a cause that was not legislated at all. If an unlegislated causation is invalid, then likewise what was legislated if it is taken for something it was not legislated for. A third aspect is that the fact that the legislator did not legislate this cause for this specific effect is evidence that this causation contains a harm and not a benefit, or that the benefit for which the cause was legislated is negated by that effect, making the cause in relation to it mere frivolity." (1) "The third: it is when the cause is intended for an effect that is neither known nor presumed to be intended by the legislator or not intended at all. This is a point of consideration and a matter of ambiguity and confusion. This is because if we were to cause it, it could be that this cause is not designated for this presumed effect, just as it could be designated for it and for others. In the first case, the causation would be unlegislated. In the second case, it would be legislated. If the action is in a state of uncertainty between being legislated or unlegislated, then proceeding with the causation is unlegislated." (2)
