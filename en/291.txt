When the obligation is established with what entails hardship, the solution is that not everything termed hardship is considered hardship in Islamic law; that is, these hardships are not the meaning that the principle of alleviating hardships refers to, from one perspective. From another perspective, if the obligation falls on something that entails hardship which the legislator intended to alleviate, then two principles come into conflict: the principle of compliance and the principle of alleviating hardship. Here, one must weigh the interests and harms, and the ruling will be based on what predominates in the situation, meaning that one must weigh the demand for action due to the benefit of compliance against the demand for abstention due to the harm of hardship.
Usual hardship is not termed hardship:
... It has been established through induction that every obligation involving hardship, no matter how slight, indicates a definitive general principle that the legislator imposes obligations that entail hardship. Here, two principles come into conflict: the principle of alleviating hardships and the principle of acting upon what entails hardship. Al-Shatibi saw the solution in that not every hardship is considered hardship in Islamic law. What is the criterion for what is considered and what is not? Al-Shatibi states: "There is no dispute that the legislator intends to impose obligations that entail some cost and hardship, but it is not usually termed hardship, just as the pursuit of livelihood through various trades and crafts is not usually termed hardship because it is a possible and customary practice that does not prevent the costs associated with it from being carried out in the usual manner. Rather, rational people and those accustomed to such practices consider those who refrain from it to be lazy and condemn them for it. Likewise, the usual obligations" (1). He adds in clarifying the required criterion: "To this meaning returns the distinction between hardship that is not usually considered hardship and that which is considered hardship, which is that if the action leads to its discontinuation or to some of it, or to a disruption in the individual, their wealth, or their condition, then the hardship here is outside the usual. If it does not involve any of that in the usual case, it is not usually considered hardship, even if it is termed a cost" (2). He then said: "Based on this, one should understand the obligation and what it entails of hardship" (3). He then went further, rejecting the notion that usual hardships are termed hardship, thus eliminating the conflict between the two principles. He said: "What results from ordinary actions is not usually termed hardship. The conclusion is that obligations involving customary matters and those of their kind do not entail hardship, as previously stated; what results from the obligation is not termed hardship" (4).
