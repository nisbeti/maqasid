This is unlike the permissible and praiseworthy differences. He said: "We found that the companions of the Messenger of Allah (peace and blessings be upon him) after him differed in the rulings of religion, yet they did not separate nor become sects because they did not depart from the religion; rather, they differed in what they were permitted to do in terms of exercising opinion (ijtihad) and deriving rulings from the Book and the Sunnah in matters for which they found no text. Their opinions differed in this regard, and they were praiseworthy because they exerted effort in what they were commanded" (1).

The sign of valid ijtihad:

It is clear that the one who possesses valid ijtihad is the one who comprehends the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-sharia) in their entirety and is capable of deriving rulings from the texts based on his understanding of them. The question that arises is how it is known that he has attained this rank. Al-Shatibi says: "The consideration remains regarding the extent to which one must reach in this matter to be addressed with the obligation of ijtihad as Allah has shown him" (2). What appears from the examination of al-Shatibi's methodology is that the sign of valid ijtihad consists of three matters:

First: The application of both the general principles and the specific cases together.

Second: The consideration of the consequences of actions.

Third: The consideration of the conditions of the obligated individuals in particular.

These three matters stem from the understanding of the objectives, and they must manifest during the process of deriving rulings, which is the fruit of understanding. As for the first, it has been clarified previously, and understanding the objectives is necessary for it; thus, one who does not understand them cannot apply them, and one who understands the general principles cannot do without the specific cases. Therefore, understanding the objectives includes understanding the objectives of the detailed rulings. For example, applying the principle of preserving wealth, which is a general principle, in the act of theft, usurpation, or fraud without considering the specific texts related to these actions is a mistake, as the punishments of amputation, execution, imprisonment, or others all revert to the general principle. This is a clear error; thus, it is not sufficient to apply the general principle alone. Likewise, it is not sufficient to apply the specific cases without considering the general principles; this, as has been shown a little earlier, is an entry point for destruction in the Sharia, and the idea of objectives refutes it.
