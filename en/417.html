Al-Shatibi says: "If that is the case, and the particulars (1) are the foundations of the Sharia, then what is beneath them is derived from those general foundations, just as particulars relate to their generals in every type of existing thing. Therefore, it is necessary to consider those particulars in light of these generals when applying the specific evidences from the Book, the Sunnah, consensus, and analogy, for it is impossible for those particulars to be independent of their generals. Thus, one who takes a text, for example, in a particular while disregarding its general has erred, and just as one who takes it for a particular while disregarding its general is mistaken, so too is one who takes the general while disregarding its particulars. The explanation of this is that acquiring knowledge of the general is only through the presentation of particulars and their examination. The general, in its essence, is not known to us before knowledge of the particulars, and it does not exist in the external world; rather, it is contained within the particulars as established in the rational sciences. Therefore, to stand with the general while disregarding the particular is to stand with something that has not yet been known without knowledge of the particular. The particular is the manifestation of knowledge of it. Furthermore, the particular is not designated as a particular except because the general is fully realized in it and is its basis. Thus, disregarding the particular as such is, in reality, disregarding the general itself, and that is a contradiction" (2). For the sake of preserving life, for example, which is a general principle, it is necessary to apply the particulars that indicate the permissibility of consuming pork or carrion in cases of necessity, or uttering disbelief under coercive duress, and the permissibility of redeeming one's life with wealth, and the prohibition of redeeming it with the life of another Muslim or by revealing secrets and plans that expose Muslims to defeat and extermination.

(1) The designation of particulars as foundations applies to what is particular in relation, but it is a general principle, such as the obligation of prayer, zakat, and the other five foundational rules in religion. All of these, according to al-Shatibi, are definitive foundations, but they are particular in relation to the principle of preserving religion, and what is beneath them are the particulars that are the sources of these rules.

(2) Al-Shatibi, al-Muwafaqat, 3/3.
