Since the establishment of the cause is akin to the establishment of the effect in both legal and rational matters, the legality of the cause indicates the legality of the effect. The prohibition of the cause indicates the illegality of its effect. Thus, the demand for action indicates that its outcome is beneficial, while the prohibition of action indicates that its outcome is harmful, except that the obligation pertains to the cause and not to the effect. What is required legally is to perform the action or to refrain from it, not to aim for the attainment of benefit or the avoidance of harm, because the attainment or avoidance is an effect, and it is not within the capacity of the obligated person to establish or remove it as an effect; to impose an obligation regarding it is to impose an obligation regarding what is beyond one's ability. In brief: the command regarding the cause indicates the legality of its effect but does not imply a command regarding the effect itself. If it is said that there may be multiple effects of the cause, resulting from the required or permitted action what is intended to be attained legally, and also resulting what is unintended or what is desired to be removed, the Shatibi responds that the legality of the action indicates the legality of its effect, but it does not necessitate the legality of everything that results from or is caused by it. The required action may produce what is desired to be removed, and refraining from what is prohibited may eliminate what is desired or legally permissible to exist. He states: "The legality of causes does not necessitate the legality of effects, even though the correlation between them is usually valid. This means that if a legal ruling of permissibility or prohibition or other rulings of obligation is attached to the causes, it does not necessarily follow that the rulings are attached to their effects. Therefore, if a command is given regarding the cause, it does not necessitate a command regarding the effect, and if it is prohibited, it does not necessitate a prohibition regarding the effect, and if one is given a choice regarding it, it does not necessitate that one is given a choice regarding its effect."(1)
He then said: "The evidence for this is what has been established in discourse, that what is for the obligated person is to engage in the causes, while the effects are the actions of Allah, Exalted is He, and there is no earning in them for the obligated person."(2)
