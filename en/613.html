One of the most important principles according to al-Shatibi is that the foundations must be definitive. He said: "The principles of jurisprudence in religion are definitive, not speculative, and the evidence for this is that they pertain to the universals of the Sharia, and whatever is thus is definitive. The proof of the first is evident through the inductive reasoning that leads to certainty, and the proof of the second is from several aspects: one of them is that they return either to rational foundations, which are definitive, or to the general inductive reasoning from the evidences of the Sharia, which is also definitive, and there is no third option other than the combination of the two, and the composite of definitives is definitive" (1).

The requirement for definitiveness in the foundations is well-known and agreed upon among the imams, but there is a significant difference between what al-Shatibi intended and what they intended, a difference that gives his methodology a direction distinct from theirs. The definitiveness in the foundations that the imams referred to is not about general or universal rules but rather about the sources of legislation, namely the general evidences which are four: the Book, the Sunnah, consensus, and analogy. These are general evidences from which detailed evidences are derived and from which rules and foundations are understood. For example, the obligation to adhere to the Sunnah is established with definitive certainty, as it is a source of legislation. However, the reports or narrations that come in the Sunnah, or the reports of individual narrators, do not require definitive certainty regarding their authenticity in relation to the Prophet, but rather are taken based on a preponderance of evidence. The same applies to the meanings or implications of the texts, whether from the Qur'an or the Sunnah; it is not required for the implication of a text to be definitive for it to be acted upon, but rather it must be preferred over others.

As for what al-Shatibi intended, it encompasses what they intended and more. Every jurisprudential or foundational rule must be definitive according to him, and every meaning or principle relied upon in the Sharia must be definitive, and the meanings of the texts are the objectives of the Legislator, so they must be definitive or refer back to a definitive meaning that serves it, or be included within the definitive meaning.
