The principle of public interest (maslaha mursala) is based on the combination of two foundations: the foundation of worship and compliance, and the foundation of alleviating hardship. When a mandatory command comes from the legislator, compliance is required; if it is possible, there is no issue. If it is not possible, it may be either because it is not feasible to carry it out and persist in it, which constitutes hardship, or because it can only be performed after fulfilling another obligation. These two scenarios, if they arise in relation to the obligation, necessitate other rulings. If the matter pertains to hardship, then hardship must be alleviated through facilitation. If it pertains to the fact that the action cannot be performed except after fulfilling another, then this other action becomes obligatory just like the first action. The second scenario falls under the first, because if performing an action is only possible through first performing another, then the obligation to do it without fulfilling what is necessary for it is an obligation to do what is impossible or what involves hardship. Therefore, both scenarios relate to alleviating hardship. This is the foundation of the principle of public interest as articulated by al-Shatibi.
It is applied in necessities and needs, but not in enhancements. It is applied in necessities to actions that cannot preserve the necessity except through them, thus the action takes the ruling of preserving the necessity, which is obligation. In needs, it pertains to alleviating hardship and similar matters, leading to facilitation based on the evidence's indications. The rulings of acts of worship are excluded from its application in these contexts; thus, there is no place for public interest in acts of worship. As for what has been mentioned above regarding the foundation of the principle of public interest, al-Shatibi mentioned in his thirteenth introduction a matter, which is that the foundations and principles do not fail or break. If it becomes necessary for them to break or fail, then they are not valid. This is evident in certain instances, including what he termed "entering into actions." By "entering into actions," he means that the actions required of the obligated person must be within the capacity of the obligated person from beginning to end. If they are not, then the command to perform them is not an obligation. He states: "As for entering into actions, it is the crux of the matter and the foundation of preference (istihsan) and public interest, because if the principle leads to a conclusion that entails hardship or something that is not possible legally or rationally, it does not proceed in a straight or consistent manner."(1)
