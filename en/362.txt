As for the distinction that Ibn Rajab referred to in the previous text by saying: "due to their assumption that the intention (niyyah) is specific to the first meaning mentioned by the jurists..." it indicates that some scholars have made a distinction between a legal meaning of intention and a linguistic meaning. The linguistic meaning is simply the intention in general, and thus the intention is broader than the specific legal meaning, which is the specification of intention as the intention to comply with the command of the Legislator or the intention of drawing closer to Allah. This is what is indicated by the texts of Ibn Najim and al-Suyuti, which they quoted from al-Baydawi. This is in contrast to al-Ghazali and Ibn Rajab, for example, who only referred to the linguistic meaning.

This specification of the meaning of intention in a legal sense leads to the use of the term intention for acts of worship. However, in the case of customary actions, it is only when the intention of the obligated person is to draw closer to Allah and comply with the command. The term intention can be used here because it is general, whereas when the intention of the obligated person is for their own purposes and desires, such as the intention to acquire ownership or to satisfy desires, the term used is intention. Therefore, it is said that intentions transform customary actions into acts of worship. Ibn Najim al-Hanafi said: "There is no reward except by intention" (1). Al-Ghazali said: "There is nothing from the permissible actions except that it can hold an intention or intentions that can make it one of the merits of drawing closer to Allah and attain high ranks; how great is the loss of one who is heedless of this" (2).
