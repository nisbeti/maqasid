Al-Suyuti said: "The purpose for which intention (niyyah) was legislated is primarily to distinguish acts of worship from customary practices and to differentiate the ranks of acts of worship from one another... Abstaining from things that break the fast may be for health reasons or treatment, or due to lack of need... Giving money to others may be a gift or a connection for a worldly purpose, or it may be an act of devotion such as zakat, charity, or expiation... Each of ablution (wudu), full-body washing (ghusl), prayer (salah), and fasting (sawm) may be obligatory (fard), a vow (nazar), or supererogatory (nafl), and dry ablution (tayammum) may be for ritual impurity or major impurity, while its form is the same" (1).

Similarly, Ibn Najim al-Hanafi said: "They said: The purpose of it is to distinguish acts of worship from customary practices and to differentiate some acts of worship from others" (2).

Sincerity and Associating Partners in Intentions: Al-Ghazali said: "Know that everything can be mixed with something else, and when it is purified from its mixture and cleansed from it, it is called pure. The purified action is called sincere (ikhlas)" (3). He also said: "Whenever the motive is singular in its purity, the action resulting from it is called sincere in relation to the intended purpose. Whoever gives charity with the sole intention of showing off is sincere, and whoever's intention is solely to draw closer to Allah Exalted is He is sincere, but the custom is to reserve the term sincerity for the intention of drawing closer to Allah Exalted is He, free from all impurities" (4).

As for associating partners (shirk), it is the opposite of sincerity. Al-Ghazali said: "Sincerity is opposed by associating partners; whoever is not sincere is a polytheist. However, polytheism has degrees; sincerity in monotheism is opposed by associating partners in divinity" (5). He said: "Sincerity and its opposite both occur in the heart; their place is the heart, and this occurs in intentions and purposes" (6).
