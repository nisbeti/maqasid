Then he said: "The inference of this meaning in the Sharia is conclusive, and if that is the case, the causes that one is obligated to act upon fall under the generality indicated by reason and revelation. Thus, the causes are what the servants' gains are attached to, rather than the effects. Therefore, the obligation and its address only relate to what is acquired, while the effects are excluded from the address of obligation because they are not within their capacity. If they were to be addressed, it would be an obligation to do what is beyond one's ability, which is not the case" (1).
Accordingly, the primary aim of the command and prohibition is to carry out what is commanded and to refrain from what is prohibited, regardless of the effects and outcomes, as they are not in the hands of the obligated but rather in the hands of the Legislator. Thus, when the obligated person performs an action, they should consider its ruling and adhere to that ruling, rather than looking at its cause to determine whether it is beneficial or harmful (2). He says: "It is not necessary for the obligated person, in engaging in the causes, to pay attention to the effects or to intend them; rather, the aim is to operate under the established rulings, whether they are causes or not, whether they are justified or not. The evidence for this is what has been previously mentioned, that the effects return to the one who causes them, and they are not within the capacity of the obligated. If that is the case, then what is necessary is to consider what pertains to their own acquisition, while what is beyond that is not necessary and is what is sought" (3). Furthermore, among the legal obligations, there are those that have a share for the self and to which there is an inclination, which prevents one from entering under the requirement of the demand (4)" (5).
