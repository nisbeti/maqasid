He said: "If it is not outside the norm but occurs in a manner similar to the hardship that arises in ordinary tasks, then the legislator, even if he did not intend for it to occur, does not intend to remove it either. The evidence for this is that if he had intended to remove it, the obligation would not remain, since every ordinary or extraordinary task entails effort and obligation to a degree, whether little or much" (1).
... Along with these hardships is the hardship of opposing one's desires, which is not taken into account in obligations because one of the objectives of the legislator is to divert the obligated person from following their desires to compliance. He said: "Opposing what the souls desire is difficult for them, and it is hard to depart from it" (2). He said: "But the legislator intended by establishing the Sharia to divert the obligated person from following their desires so that they become a servant of Allah. Therefore, opposing one's desires is not one of the hardships considered in obligations, even if it is difficult in the course of habits, for if it were considered to the extent that alleviation would be legislated for it, that would contradict the purpose for which the Sharia was established, and that is invalid" (3).
