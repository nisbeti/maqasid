However, I will point out here what is necessary for the completion of this topic. Al-Shatibi says: "The obligations of the Sharia (Islamic law) relate to preserving its objectives in creation, and these objectives fall into three categories: the first is that they are necessary, the second is that they are needed, and the third is that they are embellishments" (1). Any objective in any of these categories is not established unless it is general and definitive, that is, universal, and not particular or speculative. He states: "The principles of jurisprudence in religion are definitive, not speculative, and the evidence for this is that they pertain to the universals of the Sharia, and whatever is thus is definitive" (2). He also says in the same context: "By universals here, I mean the necessary, the needed, and the embellishments."
His clarification of what he means by universals and his stipulation of definitiveness for them emphasizes the requirement of definitiveness for the objectives in his view. He dismisses the notion that the principles which require definitiveness are the general evidences: the Qur'an, the Sunnah, consensus, and analogy. Rather, he states that these definitive principles pertain to the universal objectives, which are necessary, needed, or embellishments.
Therefore, these definitions of the objectives are limited and incorrect. The more accurate statement—Allah knows best—is that "the objectives are the ends, and the objectives of the legislator according to al-Shatibi are the ends indicated by actions, texts, and rulings as being desired to occur." Thus, actions and divine commands have specific ends, and the legal texts or the discourse surrounding them have ends that are understanding and obligation. The rulings have ends that are adherence to them and not adhering to others, and some—not all—of what results from this adherence.
