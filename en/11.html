Charlemagne, the king of the Franks, attempted, in collaboration with the rulers of the Spanish cities, to eliminate the emirate of Abd al-Rahman, but he failed. Abd al-Rahman revitalized and developed his emirate, encouraging the sciences and arts, agriculture, and industry, so that Córdoba became the capital of al-Andalus. Civil and architectural manifestations were evident through its mosques, institutes, palaces, and gardens. Development continued after him, and the civilization of al-Andalus was reflected in the active intellectual and cultural movement. Córdoba received special attention in terms of architecture, beauty, the construction of hospitals, houses of knowledge, and places of worship. Sciences and knowledge spread, seminars and intellectual and philosophical discussions were held, translation flourished, and al-Andalus thrived, enjoying security and stability. Many Spaniards embraced Islam, and among them emerged writers who contributed to the dissemination of the Arabic language (1).

Then, weak princes succeeded to the rule, and weakness spread within the body of the state for sixty-two years, until Abd al-Rahman al-Nasir assumed power in the year 300 AH. During his reign, al-Andalus reached its golden age, prosperity prevailed, and numerous construction, agricultural, and industrial projects were realized, in addition to the mosques, schools, and hospitals that were established. Palaces were built, streets were illuminated with lanterns, lush gardens spread, and students from all over al-Andalus came to study in its institutes and universities. The wealth of the country increased, and the state's resources multiplied. Much was spent and much was saved. Abd al-Rahman al-Nasir's rule lasted fifty years, from 300 to 350 AH, and he called himself Caliph (2).

Abd al-Rahman al-Nasir was succeeded by his son al-Hakam, who ruled for 16 years, during which he focused on the development of the country. He was then followed by weak rulers who handed over the rule to others, losing it, and ambitious individuals descended upon them, leading to the fall of the caliphate in the year 417 AH. Small states emerged in the cities, and their rulers were known as the kings of the taifa.
