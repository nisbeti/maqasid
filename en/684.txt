So why did he not use these answers or refer to them to refute the arguments of the prohibitors? Especially since they undermine his methodology, not just the validity of the ijtihad (independent juristic reasoning) of the one in this rank. And why did he not refute the arguments of the permissors, especially since they assert the validity, indeed the necessity, of relying on the general principles over the particulars? Instead, he became confused and perplexed by the matter and refrained from accepting or rejecting either of the two opinions, preferring to leave the issue ambiguous and under consideration. He stated that the matter, according to true consideration, remains problematic. The least that can be affirmed is that considering the matter as open to discussion and problematic is a doubt regarding what has been established for him, which is the necessity of applying both particulars and general principles together, indicating his acceptance of the validity of applying general principles while neglecting the particulars. This is a dreadful outcome for the methodology and leads to its invalidation.
Other observations:
There are other observations and objections to al-Shatibi's methodology, such as his rejection of the specification of general principles and their restriction, and his rejection of exceptions to them, as well as his statements regarding what is permissible (mubah) and the concession (rukhsah).
For instance, what is permissible is what the legislator has allowed between action and inaction, which contradicts the principle that the Sharia was established for the interests of the servants. This principle, according to his methodology, is a general and definitive one that does not change or falter; otherwise, it would not be general nor would it be a principle. Permissibility represents an equality between the desire for action and the desire for inaction, and according to the principle, the desire for action implies that there is an objective for the legislator in the action, while the desire for inaction implies that there is a harm resulting from the action, which the legislator intends to remove. Thus, the concept of permissibility contradicts this principle, which undermines the entire methodology. Therefore, al-Shatibi concluded that permissibility in this sense is permissibility in the particular. Permissibility in the particular must either be required in the whole or prohibited in the whole. Thus, he denies the existence of permissibility in the whole. This is merely an attempt to correct his idea or patch its shortcomings. In any case, this problem remains since permissibility in the particular is an existing legal ruling, which contradicts the idea of it being a general principle that does not change and is not subject to exceptions.
